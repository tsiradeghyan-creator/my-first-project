# Books: Walter Murch

Two books of his own on the craft, one book-length conversation with him, one book about his method, and a book of translations. Built from what the 94 sources say about them; book text was not fetched and is not reproduced. Summaries are in my own words.

## In the Blink of an Eye (from a 1988 talk; first published 1992 by AFTRS in Australia; revised edition 1995 and 2001)
- **Dates and origin:** It began as a talk to editors in Sydney in October 1988, transcribed by an Australian editor and published by the Australian Film, Television and Radio School in 1992 with Murch's afterword on computer editing; Silman-James reprinted it in 1995 with a Coppola introduction (S084, an account by the transcriber, not Murch). He himself dates it to 1991: "Blink was first published in 1991" (S063). A 2005 bio lists the 2001 edition (S002).
- **Core thesis:** Asks why cuts work at all, when nothing in waking vision jumps like that, and answers that a good cut follows the audience's emotion and the rhythm of its thought. Emotion comes first, and the editor's method exists to protect it (S021, S063). He tells the origin of the blink idea at length in 2016: cut marks on The Conversation kept landing near Gene Hackman's blinks, and at dawn he read John Huston calling the cut a blink (S088).
- **Key frameworks:** The Rule of Six with its tongue-in-cheek weights (emotion 51, story 23, rhythm 10, eye trace 7, the 2D plane 5, 3D space 4) and the advice to drop items from the bottom up (S063, S103). The blink as the punctuation of a completed thought, with the cut placed just before it and never on it (S088). The editor as the audience's ombudsman (named in S076). Dreams as the reason audiences accept cuts (S067, S013). The introduction (Bergman on Stravinsky) frames the advice as cautions he gives himself (S002).
- **Misreading to avoid:** one editor reports a director who took "emotion first" to mean the director's own feelings; the rule means the emotion on the screen (S084, inferred).
- **Taught in sources:** S021 (he clarifies its concepts at length), S063 (weights, blink, why cuts work), S033 (Rule of Six, the Hackman blink), S088 and S103 (origin of the blink idea; the six criteria and the balloon), S020 (Barcelona neurologists reached similar conclusions on blinking), S040 (blink rates), S002 (he reads its introduction), S014 (called an editing bible), S084 (publishing history), S049 (podcast on writing it).

## The Conversations: Walter Murch and the Art of Editing Film (2002, by Michael Ondaatje)
- **Core thesis:** Long conversations between the novelist and Murch on editing, sound, Zoetrope, Touch of Evil and Apocalypse Now Redux, arguing that editing is a craft with a hidden mathematics that has no notation yet, like music before written scores.
- **Key frameworks:** Three navigation points he relies on (S067): an audience follows about two and a half thematic elements at once, a sustained action scene averages about fourteen new camera positions a minute, and an assembly should run no more than thirty percent over the target length. Dreams as the reason audiences accept cuts. Music as an emotional cutaway, and being "pickled" in a film's material.
- **Background:** the friendship came from editing The English Patient, Ondaatje's novel; an earlier long interview with a poetry magazine (1996) already sets out editing as translation and as "writing carried on by other means" (S076).
- **Taught in sources:** S014 (review), S067 (an excerpt with the navigation points and the dream-state idea), S021 (Hullfish quotes lines from it), S023 and S049 (named), S084 (Ondaatje on the book).

## Suddenly Something Clicked: The Languages of Film Editing and Sound Design (2025, Faber)
- **Core thesis:** Looking back over six decades, he treats film editing and sound design as languages still being learned, braiding theory, practice and stories from the cutting room. The title is Maxim Gorky's 1896 account of seeing his first cut.
- **Key frameworks:** Filmmaking as translation from words on a page into images and sounds in time (S024; the same idea in 2016, S103). Nodality and cutting on the moment of inevitability (S024; at length in S088). Montage as building, not subtracting (S024). Skip cuts versus jump cuts (S024). Reverb as the sound version of depth of field (S024; "worldizing" in S087). Film as an art of mass intimacy that borrows from many arts (S024, S077). It is more eclectic and speculative than Blink (S063).
- **Dates:** UK edition first; the US edition was announced for July 15, 2025, and the publisher page reprints its Moviola chapter (S105).
- **View over time:** In March 2024 he called it two volumes (S024), and S063 (March 2025) still speaks of two. Newer: a July 2025 event billed it as the first of a three-book set (S043), and a 2025 review calls it the first stage, covering post-production (S083).
- **Taught in sources:** S024 (chapter-by-chapter interview), S063 (his own lines on how it differs from Blink), S083 (a review), S105 (publisher excerpt page), S031 and S043 (events around it).

## Behind the Seen (2004, by Charles Koppelman)
- **Core thesis:** A day-by-day account of Murch cutting Cold Mountain on off-the-shelf Final Cut Pro, and what cheap software editing meant for cinema. Not written by him.
- **Key frameworks:** His working system in practice: index cards grouping scenes into sequences, standing to cut, hitting the cut point in real time during playback (S012, which uses the book's photos).
- **Taught in sources:** S002 and S063 (named), S012 (its photos); the Cold Mountain workflow it covers (cheap stations, assistants practice-cutting, shooting in Romania without Apple's support) is described by him in S068, S069, S097 and S098.

## The Bird That Swallowed Its Cage (his translations of Curzio Malaparte)
- **Core thesis:** Off-topic for business. Translating Malaparte is how he comes down after a film ends, and it confirmed his idea that editing is translation: the mental space of choosing a word is the same as choosing a shot (S103).
- **Taught in sources:** S063 and S105 (named), S095 (how he found Malaparte while cutting in Lyon), S103 (translation as recovery after a film), S037.

## Major essays in the corpus
- *Dense Clarity - Clear Density* and its companion *Womb Tone* (Transom, 2005): the Law of Two-and-a-Half, the five-layer limit, encoded and embodied sound (S001, S002, S003).
- *Stretching Sound to Help the Mind See*: metaphoric sound and how far sound can sit from the image (S004).
- His short account of syncing the 1895 Dickson film for the Library of Congress (S008; told at length in S097).
- *Walter Murch interviews Anne V. Coates* (EditorsNet, 2000): two editors on training, digital tools and cutting down films (S074).

## Books he points others to
- AudioVision by Michel Chion, for the voice heard before its source is shown (S004).
- The View from the Oak by Judith and Herbert Kohl, on how each species has its own sense of space (S002).
- W. K. L. Dickson's 1895 History of the Kinetograph, for an inventor who predicted editing before it existed (S097).
