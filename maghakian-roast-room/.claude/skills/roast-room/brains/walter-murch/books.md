# Books: Walter Murch

Two books of his own on the craft, one book-length conversation with him, one book about his method, and a book of translations. Built from what the 79 sources say about them; book text was not fetched and is not reproduced. Summaries are in my own words.

## In the Blink of an Eye (first published 1991 by his own dating; revised edition 2001)
- **Dates:** He says "Blink was first published in 1991" (S067); a 2005 bio lists the 2001 edition (S002).
- **Core thesis:** Asks why cuts work at all (S067), when nothing in waking vision jumps like that, and answers that a good cut follows the audience's emotion and the rhythm of its thought. Emotion comes first, and the editor's method exists to protect it (S017, S067).
- **Key frameworks:** The Rule of Six with its tongue-in-cheek weights (emotion 51, story 23, rhythm 10, eye-trace 7, the 2D plane 5, 3D space 4) and the advice to drop items from the bottom up. The blink as the punctuation of a completed thought, and the cut placed just before it. His interview explanation for why cuts work at all, our nightly dreaming, appears in S049, S071 and S081. The book's introduction (Bergman on Stravinsky) frames the advice as cautions he gives himself (S002), which he repeats in 2026 when he says the emotion-first list is meant for an analytical editor like him (S059, paraphrase).
- **Taught in sources:** S017 (he clarifies its concepts at length), S049 (he clears up misconceptions about the Rule of Six), S067 (weights, blink, why cuts work), S029 (Rule of Six, the Hackman blink), S016 (Barcelona neurologists reached similar conclusions on blinking), S036 (blink rates), S002 (he reads its introduction), S010 (called an editing bible).

## The Conversations: Walter Murch and the Art of Editing Film (2002, by Michael Ondaatje)
- **Core thesis:** Five long conversations between the novelist and Murch on editing, sound, Zoetrope, Touch of Evil and Apocalypse Now Redux, arguing that editing is a craft with a hidden mathematics that has no notation yet, like music before written scores.
- **Key frameworks:** Editing as an art still waiting for its notation. Three navigation points he relies on (S071): an audience follows about two and a half thematic elements at once, a sustained action scene averages about fourteen new camera positions a minute, and an assembly should run no more than thirty percent over the target length. Dreams as the reason audiences accept cuts. Music as an emotional cutaway, and being "pickled" in a film's material.
- **Taught in sources:** S010 (review), S071 (an excerpt with the navigation points and the dream-state idea), S017 (Hullfish quotes lines from it), S019 and S043 (named, not argued).

## Suddenly Something Clicked: The Languages of Film Editing and Sound Design (2025, Faber)
- **Core thesis:** Looking back over six decades, he treats film editing and sound design as languages still being learned, braiding theory, practice and stories from the cutting room. The title is Maxim Gorky's 1896 account of seeing his first cut.
- **Key frameworks:** Filmmaking as translation from words on a page into images and sounds in time (S020, S057). Nodality and cutting on the moment of inevitability (S020). Montage as building, not subtracting (S020). Skip cuts versus jump cuts (S020). Reverb as the sound version of depth of field (S020). Film as an art of mass intimacy that borrows from many arts (S020). It is more eclectic and speculative than Blink (S067).
- **View over time:** In March 2024 he called it two volumes (S020), and S067 (March 2025) still speaks of two. Newer: in May 2025 he called it part one of a trilogy, with later books on production, casting and writing (S058), and a July 2025 event billed it as the first of a three-book set (S037).
- **Taught in sources:** S020 (chapter-by-chapter interview), S057 (the braid of theory, practice and stories), S058 (Gorky, the trilogy), S067 (his own lines on how it differs from Blink), S027 and S037 (events around it).

## Behind the Seen (2004, by Charles Koppelman)
- **Core thesis:** A day-by-day account of Murch cutting Cold Mountain on off-the-shelf Final Cut Pro, and what cheap software editing meant for cinema. Not written by him.
- **Key frameworks:** His working system in practice: index cards grouping scenes into sequences, standing to cut, hitting the cut point in real time during playback (S009, which uses the book's photos).
- **Taught in sources:** S002 and S067 (named), S009 (its photos); the Cold Mountain workflow it covers (cheap stations, assistants cutting from his notes, dailies on DVD for the director) is described by him in S072 and S073.

## The Bird That Swallowed Its Cage (his translations of Curzio Malaparte)
- **Core thesis:** Off-topic for business. Translating Malaparte was his way to come down after a film ended, and it shaped his idea that the editor is a translator and that the last frame of a shot works like the last word of a line of verse.
- **Taught in sources:** S067 (named), S051 (published a couple of years before 2016; blank verse in editing), S033 and S049 (how he came to Malaparte).

## In progress

## Major essays in the corpus
- *Dense Clarity - Clear Density* and its companion *Womb Tone* (Transom, 2005): the Law of Two-and-a-Half, the five-layer limit, encoded and embodied sound (S001, S002, S003).
- *Stretching Sound to Help the Mind See*: metaphoric sound and how far sound can sit from the image (S004).
- His short account of syncing the 1895 Dickson film for the Library of Congress (S005).

## Books he points others to
- Art of the Cut by Steve Hullfish, for many editors' views organized by topic (S053).
- AudioVision by Michel Chion, for the voice heard before its source is shown (S004).
- The View from the Oak by Judith and Herbert Kohl, on how each species has its own sense of space (S002).
