# Positions: Walter Murch

Built from the extract notes on 94 sources (1996 to 2025): his essays, the 2016 Web of Stories life interview (S085 to S104, dated 2016-04), Art of the Cut and other long interviews. 38 topics, business decisions in editing and post weighted first, then ordered by how many sources support them. Each stance has a verbatim quote, source id and date ("c." marks a year taken from the text when `sources.md` lists the source as undated). Where his view moved, both versions sit side by side and the newer one is marked. Note: the 2020 to 2026 video masterclasses used in an earlier build could not be refetched, so his most recent spoken views (including on AI) are thinner here than they were; topics without evidence say so.

## Running time, assembly length and cutting down (11 sources)
- **Stance:** Expect the first assembly to run long. Up to 30 percent over the target trims by "diet and exercise"; beyond that the film needs surgery on scenes, subplots or characters. Past about two hours a film turns into something else. Cut even excellent material when length starts to tire the audience.
- **Evidence:**
  - "an assembly should be no more than thirty percent over the ideal running length of the film" (S067, 2005-11)
  - "You measure that length and then say, 'What is 30% of that.'" (S089, 2016-04) "If you want to go further than that, you can, and I have, we have, but to do that you have to actually cut out... do surgery" (S089, 2016-04)
  - "I call the 30% reduction 'diet and exercise'" (S094, 2016-04)
  - "When a film gets over two hours, it begins to turn into something else." (S021, 2020-02)
  - "It's like, you know, a 12-course meal, each course is delicious, but by the eighth course, oh, another delicious meal, you know?" (S096, 2016-04)
  - Slow or fast: "It's down partly to how much time that you have and what is your temperament." (S089, 2016-04)
- **Over time:** 2016-02, a writer reports his drop-dead length for most films as about 145 minutes (paraphrase, S009). 2020-02 (newer), the limit is two to two and a half hours because the audience's hypnosis has a physical limit (S021). Same view, stated more firmly.
- **Cases:** The Conversation 4.5 hours to under two; Apocalypse Now 5.5 hours; The English Patient 4:10 to about 2:38; Ripley 4.5 hours to about 2:25; Cold Mountain 5:10, cut by 2:40 (S089, S094, S096, S098). Directors such as Zinnemann, Zucker and the Coens assemble close to final length (S096).

## Script timing and the length forecast (5 sources)
- **Stance:** The editor should time the script before shooting; a studio should demand timing as a green-light requirement; shaved timings are worse than none.
- **Evidence:**
  - "I believe that it's my duty as an editor to time the screenplay" (S096, 2016-04) "I also time the screenplay." (S021, 2020-02)
  - "if I was running a studio, I would wave a magic wand and say, 'That's another requirement. Tell me the timing...'" (S096, 2016-04)
  - "The rule of thumb, which is pretty accurate, is that a screenplay is a minute a page." (S096, 2016-04) Writer-directors run longer: Minghella's was nearer two minutes a page (S096).
  - "the danger of that is that, as it is with any double-entry bookkeeping, you know that these aren't real, and then you have nothing to guide you" (S096, 2016-04)
  - Notes on the script are "just top-of-the-head ideas about where I was engaged or where I was a little confused when I read the screenplay" (S021, 2020-02)
- **Read:** an honest timing is the cheapest insurance a producer can buy. Inferred from S096, S021.

## Studios, executives and who controls the cut (12 sources)
- **Stance:** Control sits with whoever holds a bottleneck. Show executives finished-feeling work, find the one point where they are right and fix that, expect them to resist new ideas, and offer help rather than defiance.
- **Evidence:**
  - "POWER on a film tends to gravitate toward those who control a bottleneck of some kind." (S004, undated)
  - "it was a rule of thumb throughout the industry never to let producers or studio executives hear the final mix unless it could be screened with an answer print." (S001, 2005-04)
  - On Evans and The Godfather's score: "I'll pretend I'm the head of the studio. And I played the music for the film in my mind, thinking, is that a problem? No, that's okay" (S087, 2016-04)
  - "If they focus on some aspect of the film in a brief window of attention, and they detect a defect, they will extend that defect to the whole film." (S087, 2016-04)
  - "Welles was under no illusions as to who owned the film, but his approach was ‘I can help you make it better’." (S062, 2012-08)
  - Final cut by neglect on Return to Oz: "But it was out of indifference rather than charitable towards my personal vision." (S091, 2016-04)
  - Why Zoetrope left Los Angeles: "It was all done on a business level and we hated that." (S073, 2019-04); "As the turf diminishes, people hold on even stronger to the turf that they have and we just wanted to get away from that." (S086, 2016-04)
- **Read:** he manages buyers by timing what they see and conceding the real point, not by confrontation. Inferred from S001, S086, S087, S021.

## Cost of tools and the economics of digital post (10 sources)
- **Stance:** The real change of digital was economic: media and machines became nearly free, so you stop rationing stations and let assistants practice. Cheaper per seat, not cheaper per film.
- **Evidence:**
  - "compared to working with film, the experience of working digitally is that it's free" (S098, 2016-04)
  - "The real advantage of something like Final Cut is that for the first time in the history of film, really, the cost of the media and the cost of the apparatus with which you manipulate the media are relevantly insignificant." (S028, 2006-06)
  - "It's good to have four burners on a stove when you're cooking dinner. You can put all of them to use" (S068, 2003)
  - "Back in 1967, ten minutes of 35mm workprint cost $100." (S024, 2024-03) A Moviola would cost about $45,000 today (S098).
  - "saving time is fine in theory, but in practice it usually doesn't work that way." (S006, c.1997)
- **Read:** spend the savings on people and experiments, not on a shorter schedule. Inferred from S006, S098.

## Vendors, lock-in and adopting new tools (10 sources)
- **Stance:** Early adopter, but tests for hidden problems and asks working editors first. Keep more than one vendor alive; pick the one that listens; expect a vendor to drop you.
- **Evidence:**
  - "I just dive into it. I do a couple of days exploring, just to make sure there aren’t any real hidden snakes in there." (S027, 2015-11)
  - "This is not really a healthy situation, that there is only one system that everybody uses." (S097, 2016-04)
  - On Final Cut X: "Final Cut X is the 'rebarisation' of professional editing" (S100, 2016-04) "We want Avid to stay in business, because if they don't, what are we going to do?" (S100, 2016-04)
  - On Adobe: "We feel that they're listening to us, and actually implementing suggestions that we make." (S100, 2016-04)
  - "Apple pulled the rug out from under me in 2011" (S055, 2018-06)
  - "If you have something to say, you will find a method irrespective of the technology." (S006, c.1997)
- **Over time:** 1968: "I thought, this is great, electronic editing will be everywhere in five years." (S027) 2015-11 (newer): "And in fact I was totally wrong, it wasn’t five years before we were editing like that, it was 20 years." (S027) 2000: "I think there is a period whenever new technology comes in that you get drunk with the potential of it and you overuse it." (S074)
- **Read:** right about direction, wrong about timing by decades. Inferred from S027, S074.

## Shooting volume and more footage (8 sources)
- **Stance:** Digital makes shooting cheap and easy and moves the cost to the cutting room. More footage means removing good material and more time per editor.
- **Evidence:**
  - "It’s easier for the director, largely. And it’s somewhat easier for the script supervisor, but it’s harder for the editor." (S022, 2020-03)
  - "On Tomorrowland, the record was 35 resets within the same shot" (S024, 2024-03); a 2015 report says 36 and that he was the only editor, against four on Apocalypse Now (S077). The counts differ.
  - Documentary-style shooting: "The positive thing about that approach is that wonderful things happen that you could never plan in advance." (S090, 2016-04) The price: 270,000 feet for the 20-minute Valkyries battle (S090).
  - "You have to condense it, otherwise it's going to be too weak." (S096, 2016-04)
  - Deferred decisions: "it may be that it will now take 20 years to finish a film, because everything that you have to decide, you will decide later!" (S102, 2016-04)

## Picture and sound: one owner, one conversation (8 sources)
- **Stance:** The director should have one person responsible for the whole sound, who creates and mixes it. Picture and sound should feed each other until late, though he does not expect editor-sound designers to become the norm.
- **Evidence:**
  - "So what now is something that we call, sound designer is the sound equivalent of production designer." (S086, 2016-04)
  - "There was a specific problem in Hollywood sound in the very strict division between sound editing and sound re-recording" (S073, 2019-04)
  - "I think it’s not something that will become the norm, simply because you have to have interests in both areas." (S028, 2006-06)
  - "I don't see sound work and editing work as being entirely different from each other." (S099, 2016-04)

## Sound budget and where to spend it (7 sources)
- **Stance:** Sound is the cheapest way to add scale, so spend creatively there when the picture budget is thin. Spend real money only where the audience can register it.
- **Evidence:**
  - "If you did something really creative with sound, you could do something and do it relatively inexpensively." (S086, 2016-04)
  - "If you use it creatively, sound can punch way above its weight." (S030, 2017-08)
  - "it's much less expensive than image and it sneaks into the audience's mind sideways." (S024, 2024-03)
  - Zoetrope's biggest capital spend was mixing gear: "This investment would double, triple, quadruple its power and I think we were right." (S086, 2016-04)
  - Against overkill: "You have paid too much attention to something that the mind is incapable of assimilating anyway, even if it wanted to." (S002, 2005)

## Hiring, team size and assistants (7 sources)
- **Stance:** Hire for compatibility and give rope; let assistants cut and listen to them. Do not punish a stretched resume.
- **Evidence:**
  - "Compatibility, but that covers a multitude of things." (S020, 2020-04)
  - "it was very easy for me as an editor to allow my assistants to have practice editing." (S098, 2016-04) Assistants cut from his notes so they "can look at it with my eyes and get a sense of what it is that I’m interested in." (S069, 2003-12)
  - "Even when I'm hiring somebody and I have a very strong feeling that they're gilding their resume, I don't hold that against them." (S074, 2000)
  - A key fix on The English Patient came from his assistant Edie Ichioka (S095, 2016-04).
  - Training worry: digital separated assistants from the editor, unlike the Moviola days (paraphrase, S074).

## Working with directors: control, trust and many voices (9 sources)
- **Stance:** Adapt to each director's style. Trust given is repaid. He prefers "snowflake" directors who let every department add a facet over "black box" directors who want their private vision delivered.
- **Evidence:**
  - "Yes, a lot of trust, but the surprising thing about trust is, if you’re given all of this trust, you repay it" (S029, 2015-11)
  - "if the director is a collaborative director, what I also call a snowflake director, they encourage this" (S102, 2016-04)
  - "If a snowflake had all the time it wanted, every one would probably resemble a bathroom tile . . . so it's the process that adds the beauty and irregularity." (S077, 2015-06)
  - "Kathryn Bigelow, on the other hand, was in the editing room every day from 9 to 5. And you just have to navigate those differences." (S026, 2025-04)
  - "Ultimately, the editor is there, I believe, at the service of the director." (S099, 2016-04)

## Disagreeing with the director (6 sources)
- **Stance:** An editor is paid for ideas. Pitch, retreat, raise it again at the right moment, a third time if it matters, then drop it for good.
- **Evidence:**
  - "If it’s REALLY important to you, try it a third time." (S022, 2020-03)
  - "If I've been turned down three times, I will abandon the idea." (S099, 2016-04)
  - "My job is to be the person who challenges the director and bring the real vision out of hiding." (S039, 2013-07)
  - "if the director sees it and overrules it, that's the director's prerogative" (S006, c.1997)

## Previews, notes and reading the audience (8 sources)
- **Stance:** Previews show your blind spots, and keep previewing if you want a wide audience. Complaints are symptoms, often in code; read behavior as well as words.
- **Evidence:**
  - "What a preview does is emotionally move you 90 degrees off-center." (S021, 2020-02)
  - "the problem is not in the elbow. It’s somewhere else." (S021, 2020-02)
  - "you have to listen very carefully to what they say, because many times, they are saying, 'It took too long to get here on the bus.'" (S098, 2016-04)
  - "If an audience really doesn't like a film, they will get out of that projection room as fast as possible." (S098, 2016-04)
  - "for a film that wants and needs to be accepted by a large popular audience, it's a good idea to see where you are in that process" (S099, 2016-04)
  - Cold Mountain: "It was not working as it was written and audiences were emotionally confused." (S013, undated)

## Marketing, trailers and release timing (5 sources)
- **Stance:** He admits distribution is outside his expertise, but has seen dishonest trailers backfire and release timing sink good films.
- **Evidence:**
  - "So that end of the business is something that I don't really... I've experienced it in situations like I've just described, but I don't know a lot about it." (S097, 2016-04)
  - Ripley's love-triangle trailer: "The numbers for the first week or two weeks were pretty good for the film, and then it dropped off precipitously." (S097, 2016-04)
  - "it just shows you the difficulty of predicting many years in advance, because films have to be planned many years in advance" (S098, 2016-04), on Jarhead landing during the Iraq war and K-19 after 9/11 (S097).
  - The Conversation won at Cannes but flopped because audiences expected a thriller (S088).
- **Read:** sell the film you made. Inferred from S097, S088.

## Financing and distribution of small films (6 sources)
- **Stance:** Small and documentary films struggle without a studio; one festival break opens the doors. Most films fail, and the few hits carry the business.
- **Evidence:**
  - Particle Fever: "the film was turned down by film festivals. There was no studio behind it." (S101, 2016-04) After the Sheffield audience award: "And after that, doors opened up." (S101)
  - "maybe 5% make it. Fortunately, that 5% pays off enough that it sustains the industry as a whole" (S101, 2016-04)
  - Prestige projects moving to TV money: HBO financed Hemingway & Gellhorn on a low budget and a seven-month schedule (S100, 2016-04).
  - Self-financing buys control but not an audience: Youth Without Youth (S098).

## Casting and stars (4 sources)
- **Stance:** Cast both vertically (fits the role) and horizontally (the cast's relationships mirror the characters'). Stars let the audience project; deal-driven casting is a known cause of failure.
- **Evidence:**
  - "There's another weave which is what you might call horizontal casting" (S089, 2016-04)
  - "bad casting can sink a film. There's no question that if it's wrong, the film will not work, no matter how well everything else is done" (S094, 2016-04)
  - On studios casting for leverage: "It has nothing to do with what people thought would be right. They even knew it was slightly wrong." (S094, 2016-04)
  - "Stardom, if you could bottle it, you would make a fortune." (S094, 2016-04)
- **Limit:** he objected to Richard Gere in First Knight, was reminded he had doubted Swayze in Ghost, and gave way (S094).

## Screens, formats and platforms (7 sources)
- **Stance:** Judge the work at the size the audience will see it, or imagine it. Theater is look-into, home is look-at. Image quality matters only until emotion reads.
- **Evidence:**
  - "I had to find a way to imagine that screen to be 30 feet wide, rather than being five inches wide." (S028, 2006-06)
  - "home video, which I mean also broadcast television, is a look-at medium, theatre is a look-into medium" (S099, 2016-04)
  - "The quality of the image is almost irrelevant if it’s good enough for you to register the emotion on the actors’ faces." (S028, 2006-06)
  - Too much clarity hurts belief: "there is a limit, which we are pushing against, of things being visually too precise" (S093, 2016-04)
  - Fast cutting suits small screens: "I think it's less adaptable to the big screen, that extremely quick style of cutting." (S074, 2000)

## Remote work and shared dailies (6 sources)
- **Stance:** He has cut a feature thousands of miles from its director and made it work. What he mourns is the communal screening of dailies.
- **Evidence:**
  - Cold Mountain: "He’d look at them on his laptop and phone me up and talk about them." (S069, 2003-12) He also switched The English Patient to Avid mid-shoot from a barn in Bolinas while it shot in Italy (S094).
  - "There was a communal part to the process that’s in danger of being completely lost." (S027, 2015-11)
  - "And no one looks at the dailies any more" (S077, 2015-06)
- **Read:** remote is fine for cutting; it costs shared judgment. Inferred from S027, S069, S077.

## Music: temp tracks, composers and how much (7 sources)
- **Stance:** Music should channel emotion the drama made, not manufacture it. Bring the composer in early. For real dread, use none.
- **Evidence:**
  - "if you use music to create the emotion, rather than to modulate or channel emotion already created by the drama itself, you are using music the way athletes use steroids" (S002, 2005)
  - "approach music in films the way you would approach administering drugs" (S104, 2016-04)
  - "As a film-maker, if that's the area you want to go in, I would suggest thinking not to have any music at all." (S104, 2016-04), on films of dread such as No Country for Old Men.
  - "the music and the film start to mix up their DNA" (S055, 2018-06), on temp music.
  - Composer during the shoot: on The Conversation, themes written from the script were played to the actors (S092).
  - Wall-to-wall music can pay (Star Wars, American Graffiti) but carries long-term costs (S104).

## The editor's outside eye (6 sources)
- **Stance:** Stay off the set so you meet the footage as the audience will.
- **Evidence:**
  - "I want to protect my innocence so that I can transmit that to the audience unencumbered by my knowledge of how angry everyone was with each other that day." (S015, 2005-11)
  - "I don't really want to know how big the set is, or what it smells like, as it burdens the mind unnecessarily." (S077, 2015-06)
  - "One of the obligations of the editor is to be the ombudsman, so to speak, of the audience." (S024, 2024-03)
  - "The editor has the ability to lend a guiding hand in the direction of the story that a director, who is so immersed in the shots, may not see." (S033, 2009-10)

## Cutting scenes the director loves (6 sources)
- **Stance:** Some scenes are umbilical cords or scaffolding; once they have done their work they can go, and the film may get healthier.
- **Evidence:**
  - "certain scenes are heart scenes and other scenes are like umbilical cords." (S021, 2020-02)
  - "you should be suspicious of those very scenes and wonder if this film can ride the bike without these training wheels." (S029, 2015-11)
  - "removing that essential scene is not a diminution of the film. But it actually made the film a better film" (S095, 2016-04)
  - "So if you think something is not working, write down why it’s not working, because that might be the very thing that you have to get back to." (S021, 2020-02)

## Fixing script and shoot problems in post (6 sources)
- **Stance:** Post can rescue a film with one shot, one line or one sound. Deferred decisions have a price.
- **Evidence:**
  - "the ten unshot pages turned into one shot that we needed" (S088, 2016-04)
  - "having too many deferred decisions is like not paying your taxes for ten years" (S006, c.1997)
  - "I can't solve this problem visually, but maybe I can solve it with sound." (S024, 2024-03)
  - "For the editor, there is no future. If I can't solve a problem, the film is going to look bad, and so am I." (S076, 1996)
  - "the answer is to reframe the context in which the problem exists" (S076, 1996)

## Restoring, recutting and director's cuts (7 sources)
- **Stance:** When you reopen a finished film, follow the author's documented intent and interpret only the gaps. Small add-ons compound into new products. The first release, made in the heat of the impulse, usually wins.
- **Evidence:**
  - Touch of Evil (1998): "we held ourselves strictly to what was in the memos" (S005, c.1998) "Every film should have a document like this as a kind of touchstone" (S005)
  - "my mission was: do these things. And then: look at the film at the end." (S095, 2016-04)
  - Redux scope creep: "'Well, okay, if we can't do that, then we have to do this. And if we're doing that, then let's do this.'" (S097, 2016-04)
  - "I would probably go for the 1979 version, because it was made in the full heat of that original impulse to make the film." (S097, 2016-04)
  - "Kilgore tipped the balance of the film toward him, away from Kurtz" (S062, 2012-08)

## Documentary truth and manipulation (5 sources)
- **Stance:** All editing manipulates; a documentary may bend a little, never much, and every claim needs sources. Pick whose story it is.
- **Evidence:**
  - "The very fact that you do editing at all and the very fact that you choose to point the camera in this direction and not that direction is a manipulation." (S037, 2013-06)
  - "In documentary, you’re clearly obligated to not bend things too much." (S038, undated)
  - "at every opportunity, made sure that we had multiple sources for everything that is said and had visuals to back it up." (S017, 2020-08)
  - "we couldn’t pretend to take a God’s-eye-view of what the truth of this was." (S023, 2020-01)

## Credit and the editor as writer and artist (5 sources)
- **Stance:** The editor rewrites the film; in documentary the editor co-writes it and deserves writing credit. Editors are artists in effect but not in authority.
- **Evidence:**
  - "film editing is writing carried on by other means" (S076, 1996)
  - "Yes, I absolutely feel that, having done it." (S023, 2020-01), on writing credit for documentary editors.
  - "So, in that sense, editors are not artists, because we are working with a given structure that is not ours." (S104, 2016-04)
  - Prizes: "I enjoy it more when I lose" (S099, 2016-04); "It's much better actually to be nominated and not to win" (S091, 2016-04), on the "Oscar Curse."

## Burnout and the end of a project (5 sources)
- **Stance:** The end of a long edit brings about six weeks of something like seasickness. Make no big decisions in it, leave a gap before the next film, and taper off with a related craft.
- **Evidence:**
  - "It’s the equivalent, I think, to a kind of sea sickness." (S029, 2015-11)
  - "it's very dangerous to make big decisions during that six week period, because you're standards of analysis are compromised" (S103, 2016-04)
  - "If it's too short, less than six weeks in my case, you inadvertently infect the next film with the rules of the previous one" (S076, 1996)
  - "If you hammer away too consciously at a problem, you’re in danger of over-articulating the decisions." (S020, 2020-04)

## Getting fired and taking over someone else's film (4 sources)
- **Stance:** A firing is often a message to someone else ("shoot the chicken to scare the monkey"), and the replaced editor is rarely the real problem. It is increasingly part of the job.
- **Evidence:**
  - "The studio had the gun, I was the chicken, and Brad was the monkey." (S020, 2020-04)
  - "the easiest person to push down the gang plank at that point is the editor" (S103, 2016-04)
  - "I think it's happening with increased frequency in the last ten or fifteen years. It's a part of the job description so to speak." (S103, 2016-04)
  - Warning sign: when a studio says there is a better film in the material, the editor is next (S103).
  - Taking over: on Wolfman he was hired for three weeks and ended up holding a $150 million film (S099).
- **Limit:** "Maybe it was because I wasn't doing a good enough job. I don't think so." (S103, 2016-04)

## Choosing which projects to take (4 sources)
- **Stance:** Read the script first and send notes; take work that is recognizably you but stretches you; accept that turning a project down can cost you a hit.
- **Evidence:**
  - "We had lunch and we exchanged ideas, and I got the screenplay." (S098, 2016-04)
  - "You want the part to be recognizably you, otherwise it's a complete miscast. On the other hand, you want something that is going to stretch you" (S074, 2000)
  - He turned down The Hurt Locker after five war films; it swept the Oscars: "You have to make decisions based on the information that you have at the time" (S099, 2016-04)
  - Tomorrowland, started without a finished script, ended with his firing (S020, S103).

## Explaining versus trusting the audience (5 sources)
- **Stance:** Show less and leave a question open; the audience finishes the film.
- **Evidence:**
  - "And really the last creative act of any film is viewing by the audience." (S029, 2015-11)
  - "the most powerful thing you can do to an audience in a film is not show them something, but make them think that they've seen it" (S096, 2016-04)
  - "the responsibility of filmmakers is to find ways within that completeness to refrain from achieving it." (S004, undated)
  - "Films which seem overly long are usually suffering from precisely this problem." (S076, 1996), on dialogue that repeats what a face already says.

## Pacing, cutting rate and long takes (5 sources)
- **Stance:** Do not feed the audience faster than it can digest; do not cut while one shot still tells the story; single-take films stay a niche.
- **Evidence:**
  - "if you feed people too fast, they feel like the stuff is being rammed down their throat and they’re going to choke." (S022, 2020-03)
  - "you can do that, but you lose comprehensibility" (S029, 2015-11)
  - "When that shot can no longer deliver the information" (S074, 2000), is when to cut.
  - "single-take films will remain an interesting but isolated compartment within the continent of cinema" (S102, 2016-04)

## Directing and two hats (5 sources)
- **Stance:** He directed once, was fired and rehired, and the film's failure ended his directing path; he is content as editor and sound designer.
- **Evidence:**
  - "So, I was box office poison for a number of years afterwards, certainly as a director." (S092, 2016-04)
  - "I had a family to support and kids going to school, and I couldn't sustain it." (S030, 2017-08)
  - "To be an effective director, sometimes you have to be wilfully blind to certain things. And, I was not that." (S103, 2016-04)
  - 2016 (newer): "I doubt that the rainbow will come back. I don't have too many regrets about that at the moment." (S092)

## Industry cycles: exploration and exploitation (3 sources)
- **Stance:** Hollywood swings between exploring and exploiting known formulas; a medium that only exploits dies. Sequels usually disappoint.
- **Evidence:**
  - "Hollywood swings back and forth from exploration to exploitation." (S076, 1996)
  - "with a few exceptions, film sequels are disappointing: The territory is too well-known" (S076, 1996)
  - Success can trap you as much as failure: "Huge success can be as limiting sometimes as failure." (S092, 2016-04)

## Competition and entering the market (3 sources)
- **Stance:** Tools are nearly free, so the field is crowded; the edge is dedication. Wide access raises the whole art.
- **Evidence:**
  - "Film editing is now something almost everyone can do at a simple level and enjoy it, but to take it to a higher level requires the same dedication and persistence that any art form does." (S063, 2025-03)
  - "be wary of the fact that that means that many more people are trying to do the same thing you are, and it's harder to make your voice heard" (S098, 2016-04)
  - "So maybe we will have a century and a half ahead of unimagined excellence in film . . . just as we had with music from the 1720s." (S077, 2015-06)

## Work hours and physical method (5 sources)
- **Stance:** Long hours come with the job. Stand to edit, walk, take breaks.
- **Evidence:**
