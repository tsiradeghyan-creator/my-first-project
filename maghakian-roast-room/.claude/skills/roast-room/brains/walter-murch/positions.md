# Positions: Walter Murch

Built from the five batch notes on 79 sources (interviews, masterclasses, articles and his own essays, 1996 to 2026). 39 topics, ordered by how many sources support them, with business decisions in editing and post-production weighted first among equals. Each stance has a verbatim quote, source id and date (YouTube upload date for videos; "c." marks a year taken from the text when `sources.md` lists the source as undated). Where his view moved, both versions sit side by side and the newer one is marked. Topics with no evidence say so.

## Restoring, recutting and the director's written intent (10 sources)
- **Stance:** When you reopen a finished film, follow the author's documented intent and interpret only the gaps; new material must rebalance the structure, not just add. Leave your own finished work alone.
- **Evidence:**
  - Touch of Evil (1998): "we held ourselves strictly to what was in the memos" (S078, c.1998) "At that point I had to interpret what Welles might have wanted." (S080, c.1999)
  - "Every film should have a document like this as a kind of touchstone" (S078, c.1998)
  - Apocalypse Now Redux: "The new scenes balance things in a structural sense." (S066, 2012-08) but "Kilgore tipped the balance of the film toward him, away from Kurtz" (S066, 2012-08)
  - Scope creep: "it evolved from a very small seed a DVD extra for France only into Apocalypse Now redux" (S050, 2020-03)
  - Restoring a cut moment for Apocalypse Now Final Cut: "and so you've removed the very thing that was predicted at the beginning of the film" (S050, 2020-03)
  - His own work: "I keep myself from saying those things or thinking those things." (S056, 2022-02) After remastering The Conversation: "I don’t think my approach has fundamentally changed." (S016, 2020-04)
- **Read:** a written brief outlives its author and settles arguments later; a recut is a structural decision with losses as well as gains. Inferred from S002, S017, S033, S050, S052, S066, S078, S080.

## Running time, assembly length and cutting down (9 sources)
- **Stance:** Expect the first assembly to run long. Up to 30% over the target trims by diet and exercise; beyond that the film needs surgery. Past about two hours a film turns into something else. Cut even excellent material when length starts to tire the audience.
- **Evidence:**
  - "an assembly should be no more than thirty percent over the ideal running length of the film" (S071, 2005-11)
  - "my rule of thumb is that if my first assembly is thirty percent longer than the release of the film then it's easy to get to that time" (S050, 2020-03) "if it's more than 30% you have to do surgery" (S050, 2020-03)
  - "When a film gets over two hours, it begins to turn into something else." (S017, 2020-02)
  - "It's like, you know, a 12-course meal, each course is delicious, but by the eighth course, oh, another delicious meal, you know?" (S040, c.2016)
  - "it was clear that you couldn't release a four and a half hour version of Ripley. It had to be cut down." (S040, c.2016)
- **Over time:** 2016-02, a writer reports his drop-dead length for most films as about 145 minutes (paraphrase, S006). 2020-02 (newer), the limit is two to two and a half hours because the audience's hypnosis has a physical limit (S017). Same view, stated more firmly.
- **Cases:** The Conversation's first assembly ran four and a half hours and came in under two (S053, S056); Cold Mountain went from five hours to 2:25 (reporter's account, S073); Ripley ran four and a half (S018).

## Studios, executives and who controls the cut (9 sources)
- **Stance:** Control sits with whoever holds a bottleneck. Show executives only finished-feeling work, expect them to resist new ideas, and offer help rather than defiance. Read executive anger as possible theater.
- **Evidence:**
  - "POWER on a film tends to gravitate toward those who control a bottleneck of some kind." (S004, undated)
  - "it was a rule of thumb throughout the industry never to let producers or studio executives hear the final mix unless it could be screened with an answer print." (S001, 2005-04)
  - "Naturally the studio, Universal again, was completely resistant to the idea." (S066, 2012-08)
  - "Welles was under no illusions as to who owned the film, but his approach was ‘I can help you make it better’." (S066, 2012-08)
  - "when a film really previews very well, the studio is sometimes frightened because it means they have no control over it anymore." (S059, 2026-05)
  - On a Paramount post chief yelling into a phone before hearing his request: "whatever I came in to ask for, I was going to now lower my ask because I didn't want that yelling at me." (S057, 2026-10)
  - Why Zoetrope left Los Angeles: "It was all done on a business level and we hated that." (S077, 2019-04)
- **Read:** he manages buyers by timing what they see, not by confrontation. Inferred from S001, S017 (Ghost dailies), S025 (Return to Oz left alone by Disney), S057, S059.

## Cost of tools and the economics of digital post (9 sources)
- **Stance:** The real change of digital was economic: media and machines became nearly free, so you stop rationing stations and let assistants practice. He has no nostalgia for what film cost.
- **Evidence:**
  - "compared to working with film, the experience of working digitally is that it's free" (S074, c.2016)
  - "The real advantage of something like Final Cut is that for the first time in the history of film, really, the cost of the media and the cost of the apparatus with which you manipulate the media are relevantly insignificant." (S024, 2006-06)
  - "It's good to have four burners on a stove when you're cooking dinner. You can put all of them to use" (S072, 2003)
  - "That Final Cut Pro was using off-the-shelf computers, it was not using any proprietary hardware" (S074, c.2016)
  - "Back in 1967, ten minutes of 35mm workprint cost $100." (S020, 2024-03) "a Moviola, that hunk of metal, was essential, but it was like buying a car." (S058, 2025-05)
  - "we're living in paradise, comparatively speaking, because of how those those costs have simply evaporated." (S053, 2025-03)
  - Particle Fever on film "would have had to have a a budget 10 times the size of the budget and an army of people in post-production." (S051, 2016-09)
- **Read:** cheaper per seat, not cheaper per film (see the next topics). Inferred from S053, S074, S079.

## Adopting new tools and timing the switch (8 sources)
- **Stance:** Early adopter, but tests for hidden problems and asks working editors before committing a feature. The goal outranks the tool. A vendor can drop you.
- **Evidence:**
  - "When anything new comes along, my nose immediately starts to twitch." (S059, 2026-05) "I will jump into these crocodile infested waters sooner than" (S055, 2018-06)
  - "I just dive into it. I do a couple of days exploring, just to make sure there aren’t any real hidden snakes in there." (S023, 2015-11) Before Premiere he asked Kirk Baxter, Angus Wall and the Coens (S023).
  - "It’s good that there are alternate systems out there, and I had my eye on Final Cut for a number of years." (S073, 2003-12)
  - "Apple pulled the rug out from under me in 2011" (S055, 2018-06)
  - "If you have something to say, you will find a method irrespective of the technology." (S079, c.1997)
  - "forces me to look at the way that I work and examine that; it also makes me look at the material in a slightly different way." (S073, 2003-12)
- **Over time:**
  - 1968: "I thought, this is great, electronic editing will be everywhere in five years." (S023, 2015-11)
  - 2015-11 (newer): "And in fact I was totally wrong, it wasn’t five years before we were editing like that, it was 20 years." (S023)
  - Film itself: "film will disappear. It’s not going to be with us for very much longer." (S016, 2020-04) Yet analog accidents produced cuts digital would have hidden, such as the Lawrence of Arabia match-to-sunrise (paraphrase, S020, 2024-03).
- **Read:** right about direction, wrong about timing by decades; he now pairs enthusiasm with a few days of risk checks. Inferred from S023, S059.

## Hiring, team size and assistants (8 sources)
- **Stance:** Hire for compatibility, rhythm and empathy. Size the team to the film and the editor's temperament. Let assistants cut scenes and listen to them.
- **Evidence:**
  - Asked what he values in colleagues: "Compatibility, but that covers a multitude of things." (S016, 2020-04)
  - "do you want to be a film editor do you have a sense of rhythm do you have a sense of how to tell a story and do you can you empathize with characters and with the audience" (S049, 2020-10)
  - "And so listen to your assistance because there's lots of good ideas circulating out there." (S053, 2025-03)
  - "it was very easy for me as an editor to allow my assistants to have practice editing." (S074, c.2016) Assistants cut from his notes so they "can look at it with my eyes and get a sense of what it is that I’m interested in." (S073, 2003-12)
  - Team size: "But even it's down to temperament." (S058, 2025-05) Apocalypse Now had about 45 people (S058); Particle Fever was "just me and the director in an editing room." (S051, 2016-09) "So I had to gear up my assistant chops to allow me to do the work that I need to do." (S034, undated)

## Working with directors: control, trust and many voices (8 sources)
- **Stance:** Adapt to each director's style. Trust given is repaid. He prefers directors who gather many points of view over a single uncompromised vision.
- **Evidence:**
  - "Kathryn Bigelow, on the other hand, was in the editing room every day from 9 to 5. And you just have to navigate those differences." (S022, 2025-04)
  - "Yes, a lot of trust, but the surprising thing about trust is, if you’re given all of this trust, you repay it" (S025, 2015-11)
  - "He gives heads of department, and the actors, a lot of rope. Paradoxically you then feel more beholden, given the trust he has extended to you." (S026, 2017-08) "He always gives us plenty of rope, sometimes enough to hang ourselves with." (S078, c.1998)
  - "The advantage of many points of view is that the work will be multifaceted, rather than monophonic" (S078, c.1998)
  - On a future where one person thinks a film into being, the tension is "total realisation of a private vision with no compromises" (S031, 2013-07); he doubts it would be good (article's paraphrase, S035).
  - "try to do all these things, but then surprise me, too." (S058, 2025-05)

## Shooting volume and more footage (7 sources)
- **Stance:** Digital makes shooting more cheap and easy, and moves the cost to the cutting room. More footage means removing good material.
- **Evidence:**
  - "It’s easier for the director, largely. And it’s somewhat easier for the script supervisor, but it’s harder for the editor." (S018, 2020-03)
  - Resets within one take: "I had a scene with 32 of those in a single take." (S022, 2025-04) "On Tomorrowland, the record was 35 resets within the same shot" (S020, 2024-03). The two counts differ.
  - "It complicates it !! But it can complicate it in a good way." (S020, 2024-03)
  - "You have to condense it, otherwise it's going to be too weak." (S040, c.2016)
  - Apocalypse Now: "they’d shot roughly a 100:1 ratio between film exposed to film used" (S066, 2012-08)
  - Resets hurt actors, like a trapeze act repeated without a break (paraphrase, S050, 2020-03); the trend is more hours of footage per editor (paraphrase, S024, 2006-06).

## Remote work and shared dailies (7 sources)
- **Stance:** He has cut a feature thousands of miles from its director and made it work. What he mourns is not the room but the communal screening of dailies, where a crew reads the film and each other.
- **Evidence:**
  - Cold Mountain, cut in Bucharest while Minghella shot in the Carpathians: "He’d look at them on his laptop and phone me up and talk about them." (S073, 2003-12) Dailies went out on DVD (S072, 2003).
  - "There was a communal part to the process that’s in danger of being completely lost." (S023, 2015-11)
  - "It’s a way of us all pickling ourselves in the juice of the film, and that’s gone now, with very few exception." (S016, 2020-04)
  - "you haven't really seen it and you haven't seen it in an environment where you can pick up subtle mood distinctions" (S050, 2020-03)
  - He wants dailies screenings back (paraphrase, S025, 2015-11).
- **Read:** remote is fine for cutting; it is costly for crew morale and shared judgment. Inferred from S016, S023, S050, S073.

## Picture and sound: one person or a handoff (7 sources)
- **Stance:** Picture and sound should feed each other until late. He dislikes locking picture and throwing it over the wall to sound, though he does not expect editor-sound designers to become the norm.
- **Evidence:**
  - "I absolutely don't like that system" (S050, 2020-03), on locked picture handed to sound with no way back.
  - "it's a three-legged race between you, the editor, picture editor, and the sound designer" (S053, 2025-03)
  - "it was a constant feedback from editing picture to editing sound" (S050, 2020-03)
  - "There was a specific problem in Hollywood sound in the very strict division between sound editing and sound re-recording" (S077, 2019-04)
  - The sound designer as "a director of photography for sound" (S079, c.1997)
  - "I think it’s not something that will become the norm, simply because you have to have interests in both areas." (S024, 2006-06)
  - On credits: "if anyone gets a credit of sound design on any of my films my credit becomes sound redesigner" (S052, 2016-03)

## Screens, formats and platforms (7 sources)
- **Stance:** Judge the work at the size the audience will see it, or imagine that size. Image quality matters only up to the point where emotion reads. Formats differ less creatively than vendors claim.
- **Evidence:**
  - "I had to find a way to imagine that screen to be 30 feet wide, rather than being five inches wide." (S024, 2006-06)
  - "the audience is not looking at the screen. They're looking into the screen." (S053, 2025-03)
  - "the amount of sonic density that could be tolerated and even enjoyed when the image was small, dim, fuzzy, and black and white was not the same when that image was big, bright, crisp, and in Technicolor" (S002, 2005)
  - "The quality of the image is almost irrelevant if it’s good enough for you to register the emotion on the actors’ faces." (S024, 2006-06)
  - "Creatively, the differences are minute, relatively speaking, between any of these systems." (S079, c.1997)
  - Films now play on phones and 20-meter screens and are cut as if they were the same, which causes dissonance in cutting rate (paraphrase, S025, 2015-11).
  - VR: "It's like cinema was in 1897. And and we can't predict where it's really going to go." (S051, 2016-09) "you as the director cannot control what the audience is going to look at and you can't control the eye lines so to speak which is the bread and butter of cinematography" (S050, 2020-03)

## Financing and distribution of small films (7 sources)
- **Stance:** Small and documentary films earn little and struggle for distribution without a studio. One festival win can open the doors. Risk your own money boldly or not at all.
- **Evidence:**
  - "I think there's very little money in documentary filmmaking" (S051, 2016-09)
  - Particle Fever: "the film was turned down by film festivals. There was no studio behind it." (S076, undated) After the Sheffield audience award: "And after that, doors opened up." (S076)
  - Her Name Was Moviola: "we crowdfunded it for 30,000 lb" (S058, 2025-05) "We've not had any luck with getting distribution for it at this point." (S058, 2025-05)
  - On Coppola's own money: "part of you gets very conservative like I have to get my money back and Francis did just the opposite" (S052, 2016-03)
  - Timing: Jarhead (2005) was about the 1990 Gulf War, but "events overcame this film because it was about a war in the Gulf in 1990" (S075, undated); Particle Fever got its ending when the Higgs announcement came during the edit (S039).

## Metrics and forecasting post (7 sources)
- **Stance:** He runs post on a few numbers of thumb, and says plainly that they are guides, not laws.
- **Evidence:**
  - "an audience can process only two and a half thematic elements at any moment" (S071, 2005-11) "a sustained action scene averages out to fourteen new camera positions a minute" (S071, 2005-11)
  - "within 10 days of shooting I have a very good idea of how long the first assembly" (S050, 2020-03)
  - "In general, at the moment of a transition, the last shot of the outgoing scene is held 30 % longer than it would if it were a cut within the body of the scene." (S081, undated)
  - Rule of Six weights: emotion 51%, story 23%, rhythm 10%, eye trace 7%, 2D plane 5%, 3D space 4% (S067). "The values I put after each item are slightly tongue-in-cheek," (S067, 2025-03)
  - "we have intuitions about some of this but we don't have a engineering handbook for this kind of stuff" (S049, 2020-10)
- **Over time:** his split for a silent cut once sound is turned on: 2016-03, 70% fine, 10% wrong, 15% happy accidents: "But another 15% will actually be really interesting, because it happened by chance." (S012) 2026-10 (newer): 70% right, 15% wrong, 15% fortuitous: "exactly the way it should be. 15% is just wrong." (S057)

## Music: temp tracks, composers and how much (7 sources)
- **Stance:** Music should channel emotion the drama already made, not manufacture it. Avoid temp music if you can; bring the composer in early; keep the freedom to use no music; for real tension, use none.
- **Evidence:**
  - "if you use music to create the emotion, rather than to modulate or channel emotion already created by the drama itself, you are using music the way athletes use steroids" (S002, 2005)
  - "the best solution is not to have temp music at all but we don't live in that world all the time" (S050, 2020-03) "the music and the film start to mix up their DNA" (S055, 2018-06)
  - "if you gave me a magic wand I would say the composure should be on the film during shooting" (S050, 2020-03)
  - "Ideally, the amount and placement of music in a film should be such that the filmmakers have the freedom to use music or not at any particular moment." (S002, 2005)
  - "you want music to be kind of Sideways from what it is that you're looking at" (S052, 2016-03)
  - "well there's no music you're you're on your own baby and that's ultimately much more scary" (S055, 2018-06)
- **In practice:** he does temp, by intuition: "My intuitions are good." (S019, 2020-01)

## Disagreeing with the director (6 sources)
- **Stance:** An editor is paid for ideas. Pitch a real idea, raise it again later, try a third time if it matters, then drop it. The director has the last word.
- **Evidence:**
  - "My rule of thumb for places where there's a disagreement between you, the editor, and the director is another rule of three." (S053, 2025-03)
  - "If it’s REALLY important to you, try it a third time." (S018, 2020-03) "What you’re being paid for is to contribute ideas" (S018, 2020-03)
  - "it's your job, your duty to really stick up for some idea, but don't beat it to death, basically." (S053, 2025-03)
  - "if the director sees it and overrules it, that's the director's prerogative" (S079, c.1997)
  - "My job is to be the person who challenges the director and bring the real vision out of hiding." (S035, 2013-07)
  - When Coppola proposed shelving The Conversation: "i said i have a couple of ideas let's not shell the film let me do these ideas and see what happens" (S049, 2020-10)

## Before the shoot: script, timing and vendors (6 sources)
- **Stance:** Read the script before signing, time it with a stopwatch, send the director notes, and check that vendors can meet the standard. A strong script survives what the shoot and edit do to it.
- **Evidence:**
  - "I also time the screenplay." (S017, 2020-02) It takes about two days and feeds a database that predicts the assembly (S050).
  - "my technique for reading is to read at speed so I don't stop" (S050, 2020-03)
  - Notes as "just top-of-the-head ideas about where I was engaged or where I was a little confused when I read the screenplay" (S017, 2020-02)
  - "A really great screenplay has to be almost indifferent to what happens to it." (S018, 2020-03) "you can think of a screenplay as a hologram that no matter what abuse it receives, it somehow preserves some of the original elements" (S056, 2022-02)
  - "I don't want to rearrange a script everyone liked." (S029, 2009-10)
  - "Is the lab up to developing and printing over a million feet of negative to the standards of John Seale, who is an Academy Award-winning DP?" (S073, 2003-12)

## Sound budget and where to spend it (6 sources)
- **Stance:** Sound is the cheapest way to add scale, so spend creatively there when the picture budget is thin. Spend real money only where the audience can register it.
- **Evidence:**
  - "If you use it creatively, sound can punch way above its weight." (S026, 2017-08)
  - "it's much less expensive than image and it sneaks into the audience's mind sideways." (S020, 2024-03)
  - "the budget for sound will inevitably diminish" (S054, 2019-10), because shooting overruns squeeze post.
  - When the film can afford it: "when somebody fired an AK-47, it should be an AK-47 with real bullets in it" (S006, 2016-02)
  - Against overkill: "going in with a thousand lights for a single set" (S079, c.1997)
  - On syncing every footstep in a crowd: "You have paid too much attention to something that the mind is incapable of assimilating anyway, even if it wanted to." (S002, 2005)

## Work hours and physical method (6 sources)
- **Stance:** Long hours come with the job and suit a certain temperament. Stand, walk and take breaks to stay sharp.
- **Evidence:**
  - "I gravitated toward it immediately because of just something about my personality that it being locked in a room for 14 hours a day" (S053, 2025-03)
  - "Walking to work is important." (S053, 2025-03)
  - "I believe every editor should stand to edit. That’s just my particular soapbox." (S067, 2025-03) "That's why I stand when I edit, to feel this rhythm." (S035, 2013-07)
  - When stuck, switch scenes: "There's always something to do in some other area of the film" (S053, 2025-03)
  - A good editor needs endurance for 16-hour days in a stuffy room (paraphrase, S025, 2015-11).

## The editor's outside eye: off the set, apart from the director (6 sources)
- **Stance:** Stay off the set so you meet the footage as the audience will. A separate editor sees what an immersed director cannot.
- **Evidence:**
  - "I want to protect my innocence so that I can transmit that to the audience unencumbered by my knowledge of how angry everyone was with each other that day." (S011, 2005-11)
  - "So by keeping five or six steps away from that process I think I'm working to the best of my abilities." (S011, 2005-11)
  - "One of the obligations of the editor is to be the ombudsman, so to speak, of the audience." (S020, 2024-03)
  - "There is only one first time, and it is important to preserve that feeling, which is as close as you will ever get to the feeling the audience will have when they see it." (S017, 2020-02)
  - "The editor has the ability to lend a guiding hand in the direction of the story that a director, who is so immersed in the shots, may not see." (S029, 2009-10) He grants that some directors, such as the Coens, edit their own films well (S029).
  - On Apocalypse Now he was present for two of about 230 shooting days: "I did breathe the air of the Philippine location and catch the mood" (S006, 2016-02)

## Cutting scenes the director loves (5 sources)
- **Stance:** Some scenes are the umbilical cord that got the maker into the film; once their nutrients are in, they can go. Test by removing the scene that seems most essential. Write down why something fails, because you may need it back.
- **Evidence:**
  - "certain scenes are heart scenes and other scenes are like umbilical cords." (S017, 2020-02)
  - "you should be suspicious of those very scenes and wonder if this film can ride the bike without these training wheels." (S025, 2015-11)
  - "So there’s the editorial equivalent of that, where we cut out the very scene that seemed to be most important just to learn what the other scenes around it are contributing." (S017, 2020-02)
  - "the film closed over it it's it's as if it never existed" (S050, 2020-03) "it could be snipped out without creating problems later on" (S058, 2025-05)
  - "So if you think something is not working, write down why it’s not working, because that might be the very thing that you have to get back to." (S017, 2020-02)

## Fixing script and shoot problems in post (5 sources)
- **Stance:** Post can rescue a film, often with one shot, one line or a sound, but deferring decisions has a price, and the mix is the last chance.
- **Evidence:**
  - "having too many deferred decisions is like not paying your taxes for ten years" (S079, c.1997)
  - "re-recording mixers have to be sensitive to the fact that this is the last time the director can solve any of the problems in the film" (S049, 2020-10)
  - "I can't solve this problem visually, but maybe I can solve it with sound." (S020, 2024-03)
  - The Conversation with a fifth of its scenes unshot: "In the end, it came down to one shot." (S053, 2025-03)
  - "you try to minimize what seem like mistakes and maximize the potential of everything else." (S018, 2020-03)

## Documentary truth and manipulation (5 sources)
- **Stance:** All editing manipulates; a documentary may bend, but not much, and every claim needs sources and a visual. Pick whose story it is rather than pretending to a god's-eye view.
- **Evidence:**
  - "The very fact that you do editing at all and the very fact that you choose to point the camera in this direction and not that direction is a manipulation." (S033, 2013-06)
  - "In documentary, you’re clearly obligated to not bend things too much." (S034, undated) "to not bend things at all would make a film like this impossible because the physical representation of this world is so small." (S034)
  - "at every opportunity, made sure that we had multiple sources for everything that is said and had visuals to back it up." (S013, 2020-08)
  - "we couldn’t pretend to take a God’s-eye-view of what the truth of this was." (S019, 2020-01)
  - Folding two real events into one scene in Particle Fever: "And so could we fold these two things together and make it seem as if he was there?" (S051, 2016-09)

## Burnout and the end of a project (5 sources)
- **Stance:** The end of a long edit brings a seasickness that is internal and passes. Rest the mind with an unrelated craft, and stop hammering at problems.
- **Evidence:**
  - "It’s the equivalent, I think, to a kind of sea sickness." (S025, 2015-11) "you can kind of endure it again just like seasickness" (S050, 2020-03)
  - "you work with great intensity, and then it suddenly stops. And you don't know that you'll ever work again" (S051, 2016-09)
  - Translating Malaparte between films: "it was a way of sort of taking methadone after heroin" (S051, 2016-09)
  - "If you hammer away too consciously at a problem, you’re in danger of over-articulating the decisions." (S016, 2020-04)
  - Switching from picture to sound: "there's a awkward period of about a week or two weeks where you think well there's nothing to do" (S055, 2018-06)

## Explaining versus trusting the audience (5 sources)
- **Stance:** Leave one question open. A film that explains everything has no room for the audience, who finish it.
- **Evidence:**
  - "A film that answers all of its questions, that resolves itself completely on its own terms, really doesn't need an audience." (S051, 2016-09)
  - "And really the last creative act of any film is viewing by the audience." (S025, 2015-11)
  - "you have to find ways to reintroduce ambiguity. Otherwise, the film will just crush the ambiguity out of the subject matter." (S056, 2022-02)
  - On over-explaining: "And that's effective, as far as it goes, but I don't think you're getting under the skin of the audience when you do that." (S020, 2024-03)
  - "the responsibility of filmmakers is to find ways within that completeness to refrain from achieving it." (S004, undated)

## Branching out: directing and two hats (5 sources)
- **Stance:** He tried directing once and it ended his directing path; he is content as editor and sound designer. Watching others direct is not doing it.
- **Over time:**
  - After Return to Oz (1985) he kept trying to direct for three to five years, then: "I had a family to support and kids going to school, and I couldn't sustain it." (S026, 2017-08)
  - 2012-08: "No, I’m happy doing what I do." (S066)
  - 2025-05 (newer): "none of that learned experience filtered through to the visceral experience of actually directing a multi-million dollar film" (S058) and "It's a little late to be directing your very first film." (S058)
- **Also:** on doing sound and picture on the same film: "I don't think I did wear two hats simultaneously" (S055, 2018-06). Disney's neglect of Return to Oz gave him more control, not less (paraphrase, S025).

## Previews and test screenings (4 sources)
- **Stance:** Previews show your blind spots. When the audience complains about one place, the cause often sits somewhere else.
- **Evidence:**
  - "What a preview does is emotionally move you 90 degrees off-center." (S017, 2020-02)
  - "the problem is not in the elbow. It’s somewhere else." (S017, 2020-02)
  - "it was clear from the previews that the audience had had a hard time getting traction at the beginning of the film" (S058, 2025-05)
  - Cold Mountain: "It was not working as it was written and audiences were emotionally confused." (S081, undated) He added snow shots from other material to give them time.
  - A great preview can scare a studio (S059, see Studios above).

## AI in the cutting room (4 sources)
- **Stance:** Big and unknown. His working line: assemble the film yourself so you know it, then ask the machine for alternatives and keep what is good.
- **Over time:**
  - 2020-03: "yeah it's already happening" (S050), about a program that recut home movies in set styles.
  - 2025-03: "Uh I don't, as Chairman Mao said, it's too early to tell." (S053) "hold on to your hats, guys, because it's going to be another big, it's like the coming of sound in the sense." (S053) "I haven't used it other than the text AI." (S053)
  - 2025-05: "when you assemble a film yourself you learn the film." (S058) "It may be that you do the assembly and then you put it in and you say do this a different way." (S058) Risk: "AI fingerprints on it that kind of give it a certain kind of smell that is not your smell" (S058). "Now you have to in a sense reinvent the method for every new film you work on. And AI is going to complicate that in spades." (S058)
  - 2026-10 (newer): "We just don't know." (S057) "AI is television cubed because it's affecting how we fight war." (S057)
- **Limit:** he flags his own view as early: "I'm inventing stuff here because it's early early days" (S058, 2025-05).

## Will new tools save time on post? (4 sources)
- **Stance:** No. Tools let you do more in the same time and defer decisions; work grows to fill the space.
- **Over time:**
  - c.1997: "saving time is fine in theory, but in practice it usually doesn't work that way." (S079)
  - 2006-06: the economics against turning the camera off are gone, so editors face more hours of footage (paraphrase, S024).
  - 2025-03: "work expands to fill the area available" (S053), said of why cheaper tools have not made post cheaper.
  - 2026-10 (newer, and softer): in the digital era you can work as fast as you can think (paraphrase, S057).
- **Read:** faster tools raise ambition and footage, not margins. Inferred from S024, S053, S079.

## ADR and replacing production sound (4 sources)
- **Stance:** Replace bad production sound without fear, schedule it by arithmetic, and dirty clean ADR so it plays as real.
- **Evidence:**
  - "in general I schedule about eight lines of ADR per hour of recording time" (S050, 2020-03)
  - Apocalypse Now: "I'll say 92% of the film is ADR uh the sound from the set was unusable" (S052, 2016-03); elsewhere "I’m guessing 98 percent of it is ADR." (S006, 2016-02). The figures differ.
  - "I made good ADR sound not so good in order to make it sound more like production dialogue" (S052, 2016-03)
  - "so do anything you can to get rid of the microphone presence" (S050, 2020-03)

## Pacing, cutting rate and long takes (4 sources)
- **Stance:** Do not feed the audience faster than it can digest. Fast cutting in bursts is fine; as a house style it costs comprehension. Long unbroken takes have a short shelf life.
- **Evidence:**
  - "if you feed people too fast, they feel like the stuff is being rammed down their throat and they’re going to choke." (S018, 2020-03)
  - "you can do that, but you lose comprehensibility" (S025, 2015-11)
  - "And humor, such as it is, is a very good digestive agent." (S018, 2020-03)
  - "it has a limited shelf life in my feeling" (S049, 2020-10), on long-take films such as 1917.

## Credit and the editor as writer (3 sources)
- **Stance:** In documentary the editor co-writes the film and deserves writing credit.
- **Evidence:**
  - Asked whether documentary editors deserve writing credit: "Yes, I absolutely feel that, having done it." (S019, 2020-01)
  - "The editor of a documentary is helping to write the story" (S016, 2020-04)
  - "Whereas on a documentary, you are deciding what notes are going to get played in the first place." (S019, 2020-01)
  - He repeats it in 2026 (paraphrase, S057).

## Competition and entering the market (3 sources)
- **Stance:** Tools are now nearly free, so everyone can cut and the field is crowded; the edge is dedication. Start by making something.
- **Evidence:**
  - "The bad news is everybody can do that. Everybody in the world can do that." (S058, 2025-05)
  - "The good news is that the tools that you have are completely accessible to you now for almost nothing." (S058, 2025-05)
  - "back 60 years ago, we didn't have competition" (S058, 2025-05) "So, we we entered filmmaking at a stable era." (S058)
  - "Film editing is now something almost everyone can do at a simple level and enjoy it, but to take it to a higher level requires the same dedication and persistence that any art form does." (S067, 2025-03)
  - For newcomers: "short-term pessimistic, long-term optimistic" (S057, 2026-10)

## Getting fired and taking over someone else's film (3 sources)
- **Stance:** A firing is often a message to someone else, and a replaced editor is rarely the real problem. Take over only to help a friend, and not for long.
- **Evidence:**
  - Tomorrowland: "The studio had the gun, I was the chicken, and Brad was the monkey." (S016, 2020-04) "sometimes you have to kill the chicken to scare the monkey and in this case I was the chicken and Brad was the monkey" (S050, 2020-03)
  - "it usually has absolutely nothing to do with the talents of big previous editor" (S050, 2020-03); the cause is usually friction between producer, studio and director (S050).
  - "it's usually to help a friend" (S050, 2020-03) "you are the grim reaper who comes in when to take over the corpse of a dying film you know" (S050)

## Choosing which projects to take (2 sources)
- **Stance:** Three legs: the material resonates and pushes you, the director and team fit, and there is enough time and money. Any two are almost enough; a missing leg is a warning. No script, no deal.
- **Evidence:**
  - "those three things are important any two of those are almost enough" (S050, 2020-03)
  - On Tomorrowland: "if you can't give me the script there must be problems with it but I didn't say that" (S050, 2020-03) "I made a judgement and it turned out to be the wrong judgement" (S050)
  - His usual start: "We had lunch and we exchanged ideas, and I got the screenplay." (S075, undated)
