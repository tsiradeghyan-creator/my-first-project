# Stories: Walter Murch

Built from the five batch notes on 79 sources. 20 stories and cases he reuses, with the lesson he draws, ordered by how many sources tell them. Source ids in parentheses.

1. **The Conversation, rescued by one shot.** Coppola left for Godfather II with 78 of 397 scenes unshot, and the first assembly ran four and a half hours; when Coppola proposed shelving it, Murch asked to try a few ideas first: one new shot of a hand pulling tape off a reel, Meredith and Moran recast as accomplices, a wild-track line slid in at 2 a.m., and a cut from the elevator to the spinning tape reel. "In the end, it came down to one shot." (S053) Lesson: before you shelve a broken film, try the cheapest structural fix (S049, S053, S056, S058, S066, S081).
2. **The Godfather's elevated train.** With no music allowed before the Solozzo killing, he laid in distant trains that grow louder and screech, "the sound that Michael Corleone's neurons are making as he pulls the trigger" (S055). Lesson: a sound detached from the picture carries a character's inner state, and audiences accept more abstraction than you expect (S002, S004, S018, S055, S057, S077).
3. **Tomorrowland: the chicken and the monkey.** He started without a finished script, worked 15 months, and Disney let him go to pressure Brad Bird, whose 150-page script had to fit a two-hour cut. "The studio had the gun, I was the chicken, and Brad was the monkey." (S016) Lesson: insist on the script before you sign, and read a firing as a message to someone else (S016, S018, S049, S050, S067).
4. **Apocalypse Now's opening, from scraps.** Coppola gave the newest, "sanest" editor the first section; Murch built it from a napalm test shot and a Sheen acting exercise, neither meant for the film, plus a synthesized ghost helicopter. "None of the opening eight minutes was in the screenplay." (S026) Lesson: the best material can be what nobody planned to use (S004, S006, S026, S052, S081).
5. **Cold Mountain on Final Cut Pro.** He cut the first major Hollywood feature on Final Cut in Bucharest while Minghella shot in the Carpathians, sending dailies on DVD; with Avid he had rationed stations, with Final Cut he just added another Mac. Lesson: when a tool crosses a price threshold, staffing and experiment habits change with it (S017, S055, S072, S073, S074).
6. **Particle Fever: no studio, then one festival.** Hundreds of hours shot over years, a lucky Higgs announcement mid-edit that gave the film a real ending, then rejection by festivals until a Sheffield audience award: "And after that, doors opened up." (S076) Lesson: an independent film lives or dies on one break, so get it in front of an audience (S031, S034, S039, S051, S076).
7. **Julia: the umbilical cord.** A preview showed the audience lost in a chain of memories, so Murch proposed cutting the scene that Zinnemann said was his reason for making the film; Zinnemann let it go. Lesson: the scene that got the maker into the film is not always the one the audience needs (S017, S025, S050, S058).
8. **Touch of Evil from a 58-page memo.** Restoring Welles's film in 1998, the team followed his memo strictly and interpreted only the gaps; an executive had read the same memo as sabotage forty years earlier. Lesson: a detailed written intent is a touchstone that outlives its author (S002, S017, S078, S080).
9. **Her Name Was Moviola.** He pitched a short film recreating a 1972 cutting room, crowdfunded it for about 30,000 pounds, shot it in ten days, nearly failed for lack of mag stripe and edge-coding tape, and still has no distributor. Lesson: a passion project can be funded cheaply; distribution is the hard part (S020, S021, S058, S067).
10. **Minghella's long first assemblies.** The English Patient ran 4:20, Ripley about 4:30 to 4:45 and Cold Mountain 5:10 at first assembly; Ripley was fascinating and unreleasable. Low "water content" meant cutting good scenes, not padding. Lesson: dense footage costs time in the cutting room, so plan for it (S018, S040, S050).
11. **Coup 53: 532 hours and four timelines.** He built four chronological timelines of three to four hours, wove them into one of 8.5 hours, planned on colored cards, and put the director on camera as the storyteller; 30 percent of the material was in Persian he could not read. Lesson: in documentary the structure comes out of the process, so decide whose story it is (S013, S019, S067).
12. **Return to Oz.** He directed it at 40; it failed critically and commercially after two changes of Disney management, and he kept trying to direct for years until "I had a family to support and kids going to school, and I couldn't sustain it." (S026) Lesson: one flop can close a path, so know what you can afford to wait for (S025, S026, S058).
13. **Redux by scope creep.** A French DVD extra grew into Apocalypse Now Redux: "well as long as we're doing that why don't we do this well if we're doing this then we could do that" (S052). The studio resisted, and the new scenes shifted the film's balance toward Kilgore. Lesson: small add-ons compound into a new product with new trade-offs (S050, S052, S066).
14. **THX 1138 robots and the Apocalypse Now mix.** At 2 a.m. he saw that three robots' footsteps need no sync because the mind stops counting at three; years later six well-justified premixes became a racket together until he cut to five and made the music the sacrificial layer. Lesson: stop paying for detail past what the audience can register, and parts that work alone can fail together (S001, S002).
15. **The English Patient: a bomb is just a bomb.** Cutting Kip's Hiroshima scene left his change without a trigger; an assistant's remark led Murch to move the booby-trap death earlier. Lesson: when you cut a pivot, build a new one, and listen to your assistant (S017, S053).
16. **Recording the narration himself.** With Willard's narration dropped and Coppola unconvinced, Murch read the old script into a microphone and laid it in as a guide; it worked, and Michael Herr rewrote it. Lesson: show the fix instead of arguing for it (S006, S052).
17. **Romeo Is Bleeding: the flaw becomes the character.** Called in to calm a post-production fight, he found the lead actress could not play the written role and proposed making her a Russian mobster. Lesson: when casting fails, rewrite the role to fit the actor (S018, S050).
18. **American Graffiti's great preview.** 900 people went wild in San Francisco, the Universal executive called it the worst preview of his life, and Coppola offered to buy the film back for about its budget; the executive backed off. Lesson: a great preview can frighten a buyer because it takes away their control (S059).
19. **Ghost and the nervous executives.** Executives saw a seduction scene in dailies and wanted to recast Tony Goldwyn, so Murch cut the scene to cool things down; later the ending felt too harsh without it and it went back in. Lesson: remove a scene to calm a buyer if needed, but keep track of the payoff it holds up (S017).
20. **The yelling post chief.** Asking Paramount's head of post for blank leader on Ghost, he sat through a screaming phone call, then realized it was theater meant to make him lower his ask. Lesson: in a negotiation, an executive's anger may be staged, so decide your ask before you walk in (S057).
