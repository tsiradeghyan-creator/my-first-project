# Stories: Walter Murch

Built from the extract notes on 94 sources, above all the 2016 Web of Stories life interview, where he tells most of these at length. 22 stories and cases he reuses, with the lesson he draws, ordered by how many sources tell them and how often they bear on post decisions. Quotes are verbatim; the rest is paraphrase.

1. **Saving The Conversation with one shot (1973).** Coppola stopped shooting with ten pages unshot to prep Godfather II, then decided to shelve the film. Murch went for a long run, asked for one more week, split the first tape-assembly scene, had Stett want the tapes too badly, made Meredith a tape thief, and turned an unshot scene into a dream. The ten pages became one insert of an empty reel, shot with a camera borrowed from Chinatown and Hackman's brother's shoulder. "the ten unshot pages turned into one shot that we needed" (S088) Lesson: restructure before you shelve; the cheapest new element can stitch a film (S062, S087, S088, S013).

2. **The elevated train before the Sollozzo killing.** With no score allowed before the intermission point, he laid in Bronx elevated trains that screech louder as Michael decides, then cut out on the shot. A mafia killer later said it was exactly how his first killing felt. "What they register is Michael." (S089) Lesson: metaphoric sound reaches the audience through the picture and the performance (S002, S004, S022, S055, S088, S089).

3. **The horse's head cue.** Evans hated Rota's Godfather score six weeks from release; Coppola left to direct a play. Murch played the film in his head as the studio head, found the one cue where Evans had a point, and layered the A and B sections into a dissonant horror version timed to the first blood. Evans phoned Bluhdorn from the mixing stage, held the phone to the screen, and never complained again (S087). Lesson: concede the buyer's real point and solve it with craft.

4. **Apocalypse Now's opening, from scraps.** Coppola gave the "sanest" editor the opening. Murch built it from a slow-motion napalm shot meant only as a special-effects record, Sheen's hotel-room acting exercise (real whiskey, real blood), and shots stolen from the ending, cut on a modified three-screen KEM with no storyboards (S090, S076). Lesson: the best material can be what nobody planned to use (S009, S062, S076, S090).

5. **Narration in his own voice.** Told Apocalypse Now would have no narration, he judged there was no time to reinvent a film shot from a narrated script, recorded the old narration himself and screened 30 minutes; the room agreed, and Michael Herr wrote eight versions. He admits cutting Julia, which had narration, biased him (S090, S009). Lesson: show the fix instead of arguing.

6. **The Valkyries rights scramble.** A month or two before release, nobody had cleared the Solti recording, and Decca said no twice. He mapped 17 recordings against Solti's tempo, found Leinsdorf close in tempo but wrong in color against the blue ocean, and Coppola finally phoned Solti, who said "Of course, dear boy." The film uses a transfer from the LP (S090). Lesson: clear music early, and go to the artist when the company says no.

7. **The Godfather II headroom mistake.** He pushed the dynamic range for the Havana riots in a small mixing room; theaters could not balance dialogue and explosions; the track was compressed and reprinted. "And I saw my career being blighted by this terrible mistake." (S088) Lesson: test for the room the audience will be in.

8. **The English Patient: a bomb is a bomb.** Cutting the Hiroshima scene left Kip's change without a trigger. His assistant Edie Ichioka said "a bomb is a bomb" and walked out; he used the booby-trap death of Hardy instead, which also let a breakup scene become Hana overhearing the patient's confession (S095, S104, S094). Lesson: when you cut a pivot, find a new one already in the film, and listen to your assistant.

9. **Switching to Avid mid-shoot.** A month into The English Patient his son was diagnosed with a brain tumor. He offered to leave; Minghella and Zaentz kept him; he re-pitched the Avid they had refused so he could catch up, and cut from a barn in Bolinas while the film shot in Italy (S094). Lesson: when a crisis hits, propose the process change that lets you deliver.

10. **Cold Mountain on Final Cut Pro in Romania.** After Avid blamed his team for a bug that was theirs, he took a big studio film to Final Cut in Bucharest without Apple's support, backed by a small LA company; it worked, and an extra station cost a couple of thousand dollars (S097, S098, S068, S069). Lesson: keep more than one vendor alive, and line up support before you break new ground.

11. **Touch of Evil from a 58-page memo (1998).** Welles wrote the memo overnight after one viewing of the studio's recut. Murch followed it as instructions for about six weeks, doing even a note he did not understand (cutting a Menzies close-up), whose reason appeared only when the whole film ran. Ernie Nims left two boxes of memos on his porch (S095, S005, S007). Lesson: a written intent outlives its author; do the note, then judge the whole.

12. **Julia, Zinnemann and the sandwiches.** Murch made an invisible optical to remove half-eaten sandwiches from the best moment of a sailing shot; Zinnemann kept the original and asked the cameraman at the answer-print party if he wanted anything changed; he did not. "Okay, you win." Zinnemann also printed a take with a broken pan handle to teach the crew to check gear (S092). Lesson: a small flaw in a great shot often matters less than you think.

13. **American Graffiti's 42 songs and the great preview.** Verna Fields warned the songs would drown the story; worldizing them in a backyard over four and a half hours solved it. After a wild preview, the executive called it poor; Coppola wrote a check to buy the film back and the executive backed off. Then the executive called the soundtrack an embarrassment because one line was deliberately held out; putting a little back made it "fantastic" (S087). Lesson: a buyer's alarm may hang on one visible detail.

14. **The drunk executive.** At 28, he answered a Paramount executive who threatened to fire the Godfather sound team: "So you're hearing only 30% of what the final mix will be. And besides that, you're drunk." (S086) The executive agreed, and the post chief gave Murch a decade of goodwill. Lesson: defend the work with facts, whatever the rank.

15. **Return to Oz: fired on Thursday, rehired on Tuesday.** Directing at 40, with a child lead, puppets and slow lighting, he fell behind and Disney fired him; Lucas flew from Japan and vouched for him. Lucas's advice, "shoot the master," let the crew see the scene and sped the shoot. The film flopped and he was "box office poison" as a director (S091, S092, S030). Lesson: one flop can close a path; ask the person who has done it.

16. **Tomorrowland: the chicken and the monkey.** After 15 months, Disney fired him following reshoots, saying there was a better film in the material; the released cut differed by ten minutes he and Bird had already listed. "So, in this case, I was the chicken, and Brad Bird, the director, was the monkey." (S103) Lesson: a firing can be a message to someone else; the phrase "a better film in the material" is the warning (S020, S103).

17. **Particle Fever: no studio, then one festival.** Festivals rejected it as too science-y until a friend got it into Sheffield, where it won the audience award: "And after that, doors opened up." (S101) Screenings showed where audiences got lost in the physics, and the Super Collider scene moved three times before it found a home inside a lecture (S101, S100, S037). Lesson: test hard material on audiences; an independent film lives on one break.

18. **Ripley's lying trailer.** Paramount's favored trailer sold a love triangle; Minghella said audiences would hate him, and the publicist answered, "Anthony, they already hate you." The film opened well and then dropped (S097). Lesson: dishonest marketing borrows from week three.

19. **Wolfman: three weeks that became the film.** Hired to keep a sidelined director busy, he and Joe Johnston produced a cut the studio preferred, and he ended up holding a $150 million film; a fight over the score stopped all further previews, and the film disappointed (S099). Lesson: indecision that stops previews costs a popular film.

20. **Hurt Locker, turned down.** Tired after five war films, he passed on Bigelow's script; it swept the Oscars, including editing and sound. "You have to make decisions based on the information that you have at the time" (S099) Lesson: accept the breaks.

21. **Harrison Ford's "Kurtz."** Brando renamed the colonel Leighley, then came back shaved and said he had lied about not reading Conrad, so the name went back to Kurtz. Ford refused to redub; Murch took the picture away, looped the old line in his ear, and got it in one take (S102). Lesson: remove what makes the task impossible for the performer.

22. **The singing semi.** He woke at 3 a.m., rode to a freeway bridge and recorded an empty truck singing into the distance for The Rain People; he reused it under Don Corleone's funeral in place of music. "You shouldn't do it too much, otherwise people start to know what to expect." (S104) Lesson: a real sound can do what score would do, if used sparingly.
