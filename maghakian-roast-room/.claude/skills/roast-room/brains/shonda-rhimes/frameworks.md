# Frameworks: Shonda Rhimes

Built from the extract notes on 113 sources (speeches, TED talks, long podcasts and radio transcripts, trade interviews and profiles, 2009 to 2026). 24 frameworks, most-used first. "Sources" counts distinct sources where the idea appears (ids listed). Names are hers where she named them; otherwise a short descriptive name marked *(unnamed)*. The "How to score" lines are scoring guidance for the council built from her stated rules, not quotes.

Fastest route to the seat's question (will this story hold an audience across many episodes and seasons?): 1 (character engine), 2 (know the last moment), 3 (reinvent each season), 11 (the hundreds-of-episodes test), then 9 (watchable, not likeable).

---

## 1. Character is the engine, not plot *(unnamed; "you're not speaking English" is her room rule)*
- **What it is:** A series runs on what characters want, need and do. Plot is what falls out of them. She throws plot pitches back at writers and starts from a person in a specific situation.
- **Parts:** (1) Ask what the characters would do and what they need to do to move forward. (2) Reject pitches that are plot pieces "plucked off a wall". (3) Make the premise about how people feel about the work, not the work itself (Grey's is about how the doctors feel about the patients). (4) Ask why for every character choice. (5) Pick the medium that gives characters time: television over film.
- **When to use:** Any pilot, bible, season plan or pitch; any note that says "we need a twist here".
- **How to score (guidance):** 5 if every lead has a want and a cost you can state in one line and the plot grows from them; 3 if the characters are vivid but the engine is a case or a gimmick; 1 if the pitch is a chain of events with interchangeable people.
- **Quote:** "I think of story in terms of character and what characters would do and what characters need to do in order to make them move forward" (S100). "when writers come into my writer's room and pitch me plot, I say, "You're not speaking English."" (S100). On Grey's: "And it wasn't about the patients. It was how the doctors felt about the patients. It was about their lives." (S076). Why TV: "you can stay with a character for as long as it takes for them to become who they need to be versus a movie when you have like an hour and 45 minutes max" (S076).
- **Sources:** 8 (S012, S015, S034, S076, S081, S100, S101, S102)

## 2. Know the last moment first *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Before a season (or a drama) starts, she fixes the exact last moment. That moment tells her how the whole season plays. For a finite show she also knows the series ending.
- **Parts:** (1) Decide the season finale's last image before writing episode one. (2) Write the drama toward it. (3) For a finite premise, know how the series ends and how long it takes. (4) When the run outlives the plan, move the planned ending into the show and keep going.
- **When to use:** Season planning, a series bible, a pitch to a buyer who asks "how many seasons?", a limited series.
- **How to score (guidance):** 5 if the team can state the season's final moment and the series' end state; 3 if they know the theme of the end but not the moment; 1 if the plan is "we'll see where it goes".
- **View change:** 2009 to 2013 she had the end of every Grey's season and of Scandal (S089, S102). 2017: "I just always knew where we were going to end." (S100). 2018: "I have written the end of the show at least six times." (S092). 2025 (newer): "So after a while I just started writing those things that I thought would happen at the end into the show, because it wasn't ending." (S080). Scandal's planned ending moved too: the season six finale used what she had planned as all of season seven (S104).
- **Quote:** "We start every season by figuring out what the finale for that season is. The exact last moment of the season." (S089) "That last moment tells me everything about how our season will play out." (S089) "I try to step into a drama knowing, from the very beginning, what the last scene is going to be." (S087)
- **Sources:** 10 (S036, S060, S080, S087, S089, S092, S100, S102, S104, S110)

## 3. Burn the story; reinvent every season *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Never save story. End each season (and on Scandal each episode) with nothing left in the drawer, then build the next season as a new show. This is how a long run stays alive.
- **Parts:** (1) Spend the stories you were saving. (2) End the season as "a massacre" so the fall restarts the show (S067, paraphrase). (3) Treat season six as the turning point where a show sinks or moves to its next phase. (4) Keep one constant per episode (on Grey's a life saved or lost) and change everything else.
- **When to use:** Renewals, season two and later, any long-running show that feels stale, any team hoarding its best twist for later.
- **How to score (guidance):** 5 if this season spends its best material and leaves a real reset; 3 if it holds back a big turn "for later"; 1 if it is the same season again.
- **Quote:** "At the end of every season, [the writers and I] should all be standing around scratching our heads going, "Well, now we don't have any story to tell next season because we told it all."" (S081) "You're forced to innovate. There's no resting on laurels." (S081) "'Grey's' is a different show every season. I end the show, and I begin a different show every season. Otherwise it feels like it's going to become sort of stale." (S087) "Because every year I was like, we have to reinvent this show from the ground up. It's got to be original. The stories have to be the best stories we've ever told. Every season." (S015)
- **Sources:** 6 (S015, S067, S080, S081, S087, S089)

## 4. Keeper of the story *(her phrase; also "my only allegiance is to the story")*
- **What it is:** The showrunner's loyalty is to the story, not to actors, fans, the network or the show's comfort. Beloved characters die when the story calls for it; fan pressure does not steer the room.
- **Parts:** (1) Ask only whether the move is true to the story. (2) Do not read reactions while writing. (3) Read strong reactions as a sign it works. (4) Remember that an audience that got everything it wanted would get a conflict-free show.
- **When to use:** Killing or writing off a character, fan campaigns, an actor's exit, social media pressure, network notes about likeability.
- **How to score (guidance):** 5 if the decision can be defended purely on story; 3 if it is a mix of story and appeasement; 1 if it is made to please fans, an actor or a trend.
- **Quote:** "I always think my job is to be keeper of the story, not keeper of the actors, not keeper of the show, not keeper of the fans. My whole job is to just be true to the story." (S015) "So, to me, the audience can't dictate story, because the audience doesn't know what the story is." (S080) "And if you gave them everything they wanted, you might have a conflict-free show or two people staring at each other because people want the joy, the happiness." (S080) "I love that everybody has strong reactions, because that always tells me that it's working." (S080)
- **Limit:** She admits bringing dead Denny back was her own grief, not the story: "I think I was grieving." (S015)
- **Sources:** 8 (S015, S019, S042, S054, S068, S074, S080, S089)

## 5. Words versus spirit; write, then watch and adjust *(unnamed; "the factory" is her image)*
- **What it is:** Her contract with actors: she writes the words, they bring the spirit. No improvisation, no line readings. She then watches what actors did, in dailies and the edit, and changes the story to fit what she discovers.
- **Parts:** (1) Actors say every word as written. (2) She never tells them how to play it. (3) She watches cuts and finds the character (Cyrus is gay; Mellie could carry a series). (4) She adjusts later episodes. (5) She resists systems that lock a full season before shooting because they leave "no room for the actors' performance" (S078).
- **When to use:** Deciding how much to write before the shoot, actor relations, whether to keep a writer on set, streaming schedules.
- **How to score (guidance):** 5 if the production leaves room to learn from the first episodes shot and the writer sees cuts; 3 if scripts are locked but the edit is open; 1 if everything is fixed before anyone acts.
- **Quote:** "I bring the words, and they bring the spirit." (S073) "The words are not a suggestion." (S073) "I always say that I put it in one side of the factory and it comes out the other, and then I get it back and I love to see what the actors did with it." (S079) "I loved the ability to have an actor do a scene one day, and then for me to watch and go, "Oh, so that's what that story's going to be," and then adjust the story." (S080) "I don't think we've ever started shooting a show and had the end of the show written." (S080)
- **Sources:** 7 (S034, S073, S078, S079, S080, S094, S104)

## 6. The key that turns the lock (casting) *(her phrase)*
- **What it is:** Cast the person who makes the story happen in her head, even against everyone else in the room. Casting is a long marriage. Do not lock physical types on the page; let great actors surprise you.
- **Parts:** (1) Read widely, without typing anyone. (2) Say no to the safe choice if it does not unlock the story. (3) Write character descriptions without fixed looks (Bailey was written as a petite blonde). (4) Skip chemistry reads; good actors build chemistry. (5) Expect years together.
- **When to use:** Lead casting, recasting, network or studio pressure to cast a name.
- **How to score (guidance):** 5 if the lead was chosen because the writer sees the series in them; 3 if a strong actor fits the type; 1 if chosen for availability, fame or price against the writer's gut.
- **Quote:** "she didn't, as I say, fit the key that turned the lock that made the story happen in my brain" (S073) "She turned the key that fit the lock, you know, that made the story happen in my brain." (S073) "And so it's a marriage. It's a family." (S108) "For me, really great actors can have chemistry with a telephone book." (S080)
- **Story:** Everyone wanted another actress for Cristina; casting director Linda Lowy told her it would no longer be her show; she said no, and Sandra Oh walked in a day later (S073). Sandra Oh read for Bailey and she knew she was Cristina (S015).
- **Sources:** 7 (S015, S073, S076, S080, S087, S100, S108)

## 7. Normalizing (her word, instead of "diversity")
- **What it is:** Make the screen look like the world the audience lives in. Not as a cause or a quota but as plain truth, so every viewer can find their tribe and also meet people unlike them. More than one of any group, so no character is "the Only One" defined by that trait.
- **Parts:** (1) Cast and staff as the world looks. (2) Never write the identity as the plot. (3) Put more than one person of a group on screen so each can be about something else. (4) Remember that who runs the room decides who is on screen. (5) Write a show that includes you.
- **When to use:** Casting, staffing a room, period pieces, any market where the screen does not match the street.
- **How to score (guidance):** 5 if the world on screen matches the audience's world and no character exists only to represent; 3 if it is varied but tokenized; 1 if the world is narrower than the audience's.
- **Quote:** "I have a different word: NORMALIZING." (S006) "I am making TV look like the world looks." (S006) "To me that was not some difficult brave special decision I made. It was a human one, because I am a human." (S038) "because the people telling the stories are the people deciding who you see onscreen, they're the people who are deciding who are in the writers rooms, they're the people deciding on the crew" (S027) "I'm never going to write a show that doesn't include me," (S066)
- **Sources:** 14 (S006, S011, S027, S029, S035, S038, S050, S053, S063, S066, S067, S068, S084, S100)

## 8. Make what you would watch *(unnamed; at MIPCOM: "only make shows we'd want to watch")*
- **What it is:** The only audience she writes for is herself as a viewer. She does not chase what she thinks an audience wants; that is how you make bad television. Being liked is a by-product.
- **Parts:** (1) Would I watch this? (2) Does it resonate with me enough that I want to say something? (3) Do I see myself in it? (4) Then hope an audience comes.
- **When to use:** Greenlight decisions, chasing a trend, adapting to "what the platform wants".
- **How to score (guidance):** 5 if the creator would watch it and has something to say; 3 if it is a competent version of a hit; 1 if it is built from audience guesses.
- **Quote:** "only make shows we'd want to watch." (S011) "I tell stories that resonate with me and hope that an audience wants to see them. I don't tell stories that I think an audience wants to see because that's how you make bad television." (S033) "I do need people to like it, but I don't do my job so that people will like it." (S074) "I literally was just writing something I really wanted to watch." (S015)
- **Sources:** 8 (S011, S015, S033, S053, S066, S074, S076, S080)

## 9. Watchable, not likeable *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Leads do not need to be sympathetic in the old network sense. They need to be watchable: flawed, driven, allowed to be selfish, powerful and greedy like male characters. The show does not judge them; the audience does.
- **Parts:** (1) Give women leads the full range men get. (2) Make them flawed in a way that feels real. (3) Refuse to judge the affair, the abortion, the grift on screen. (4) Let viewers root against their better judgment.
- **When to use:** Network or buyer notes that a lead is "unlikable", morally gray leads, antiheroes.
- **How to score (guidance):** 5 if the lead is compelling enough that the audience follows her through bad choices; 3 if softened to be liked; 1 if flat or perfect.
- **Quote:** "I wasn't really interested in whether or not the characters felt sympathetic in traditional ways." (S102) "So I wanted her to feel watchable" (S102) "I didn't see any women who were fully rounded in three-dimensional, who got to be selfish, who got to be powerful, who got to be greedy" (S076) "WE don't judge the affair in our portrayal of it, but the audience definitely should." (S081)
- **Sources:** 7 (S015, S035, S076, S081, S094, S102, S109)

## 10. The one theme: not being alone *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Every show she writes is about the fear of being alone and the search for your person. Underneath genre, this is what holds viewers.
- **Parts:** (1) Find who the lead is afraid of losing or never finding. (2) Make the show a "campfire" that tells viewers they are not alone. (3) Mirror the lead's loneliness in other characters.
- **When to use:** Finding the spine of a pitch, testing whether a premise has emotional pull beyond its hook.
- **How to score (guidance):** 5 if the lead's loneliness or longing is clear and universal; 3 if present but buried under the procedure; 1 if absent.
- **Quote:** "I don't know if anyone has noticed but I only ever write about one thing: being alone." (S006) "Literally every, every show I've ever written is about people's fear of being alone in some way, or people trying to find someone so they're not alone." (S095) "sort of the gathering around the campfire to discuss the things that tell each one of us that we are not alone in the world" (S100)
- **Sources:** 4 (S006, S038, S095, S100)

## 11. The hundreds-of-episodes test and the finite engine *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Before committing, she checks how much story the premise holds. A good series idea makes her feel she could write hundreds of episodes; a finite premise has a count (Bridgerton: one sibling per season, eight seasons). Network TV needs a renewable engine; streaming can close a story.
- **Parts:** (1) Spend time with the world (four hours with Judy Smith). (2) Ask if you can see hundreds of episodes or a clear count. (3) Seed future seasons early (other siblings). (4) Do not make a season the story does not need.
- **When to use:** Series versus limited series, how many seasons to pitch, spinoff decisions, renewals.
- **How to score (guidance):** 5 if the engine renews itself (cases, interns, siblings) or the count is fixed by the material; 3 if one strong season with no clear second; 1 if the premise is one story stretched.
- **Quote:** "And four hours later, I was like, oh, my God, I can write hundreds of episodes of this show." (S015) "The Bridgerton series is eight children. Violet Bridgerton and all eight of her children married. So every season is a child," (S050) "Because you really have to start seeding in the other siblings, and what's going on with them, to push them to the next season." (S094) "I don't want to tell a story that doesn't need to be told" (S032)
- **Sources:** 6 (S015, S032, S050, S094, S095, S102)

## 12. Know exactly what the show is (the specific detail) *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** A category ("a medical show") is thousands of shows. Commit when one concrete detail, line or set of scenes makes the show specific and she can see it. If she can already picture scenes, that is the signal.
- **Parts:** (1) Find the detail that seals it (a resident who cannot shave her legs in hospital showers; "It's handled"). (2) Picture scenes before plotting. (3) Write those scenes first and build around them. (4) Drop the idea if the scenes fall flat. (5) Tell friends a few facts and watch their faces.
- **When to use:** Choosing among projects, optioning material, early development.
- **How to score (guidance):** 5 if the creator can name the defining detail and several scenes; 3 if the concept is clear but generic; 1 if it is a category.
- **Quote:** "But you can say, I want to write a medical show. And that's 15,000 different kinds of shows, for sure. So it's what makes this the specific show that you're writing." (S015) "I knew exactly what the show was," (S035) "which is a very clear indicator." (S035) "And I try to write those scenes. And then I fill in the story around those scenes." (S076) "The more facts I can tell, the more I know I'm interested in it" (S019)
- **Sources:** 7 (S015, S019, S035, S049, S076, S080, S101)

## 13. Problems, not solutions (how she takes notes) *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** A note signals a misunderstanding even when the note itself is wrong. She wants executives to tell her what is not working and leave the fix to her. She teaches executives this explicitly.
- **Parts:** (1) Ask: what confused you, what did you see? (2) Refuse prescribed fixes. (3) Fix it yourself; that is the creative job. (4) Show the edit to one blunt, unimpressed viewer (her sister Delores) before the buyer.
- **When to use:** Network or platform notes, a co-producer's demands, internal reviews.
- **How to score (guidance):** 5 if the note process separates diagnosis from fix and the writer owns the fix; 3 if notes arrive as solutions but are negotiable; 1 if the buyer writes the show.
- **Quote:** "I really believe that there is value in a note. The note itself might be wrong, but it does signal that there's some misunderstanding somewhere" (S019) "For me, it's don't walk into my office and hand me your solutions. I just need to know what the problems are" (S019) "My job is to make the story, your job is to tell me what doesn't work for you." (S033) "don't tell me the solution. The solution is my problem as the creative. Tell me what your problem is. Tell me what's not working for you." (S101)
- **Limit:** In network days she refused to read notes at all, "not totally the most mature way to go" (S019).
- **Sources:** 6 (S019, S033, S073, S081, S089, S101)

## 14. Different engines: network versus streaming *(her phrase)*
- **What it is:** Network TV is a train: a script every eight days per show, 22 to 24 episodes, fixed length, a brand for that network's audience, and a "no" you have to work around. Streaming starts with "yes", lets you write the whole season first, set any length, close a love story, and write for the world. Each needs a different story shape.
- **Parts:** (1) Name the engine before shaping the story. (2) Network: renewable conflict ("15 years of why a couple has to be apart"), cases, many episodes. (3) Streaming: closed arcs, fewer episodes, a global audience, longer gaps. (4) Speed up streaming with permanent sets.
- **When to use:** Choosing a buyer, adapting a format, deciding season length and structure.
- **How to score (guidance):** 5 if the story's shape matches the outlet's engine; 3 if it fits with changes; 1 if a closed story is pitched as an open-ended run, or the reverse.
- **Quote:** "They're completely different engines," (S042) "That's what I meant by laying track for a train, because that train really is coming no matter what, and you have to have the track there." (S019) "In network television, you have to come up with 15 years of why a couple has to be apart," (S041) "their specific audience is the world, which is fascinating" (S019) "it's very counterintuitive to the way I learned to make television" (S094)
- **Sources:** 11 (S019, S041, S042, S055, S076, S080, S085, S086, S094, S111, S035)

## 15. Creative within the fences; the shot-for-shot test *(her phrase; the test is unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Constraints breed invention (va-jay-jay came from a standards limit). When standards object, show them the identical scene they already approved with different people, which proves the objection is to the people, not the scene.
- **Parts:** (1) Accept real guardrails and work inside them. (2) Tell stories that make them nervous in ways they cannot forbid. (3) Shoot sensitive scenes shot for shot like approved ones. (4) Decline notes you disagree with, politely.
- **When to use:** Censorship, broadcaster standards, nervous co-producers, sensitive subjects.
- **How to score (guidance):** 5 if the team has a plan to tell the hard story inside the rules; 3 if they drop it or water it down; 1 if they fight on the wrong ground or self-censor before asking.
- **Quote:** "because we have the fences, they make for very creative moments" (S073) "Which meant telling a lot of stories that made them nervous but in ways that they couldn't actually say shouldn't be done." (S019) "So it's not the scene, it's the people having the sex." (S015) "Many times I say, that's a lovely note and I decline to take it." (S073)
- **Sources:** 5 (S012, S015, S019, S073, S080)

## 16. Not the issue episode *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Put a hard subject inside a character the audience already loves, inside an ordinary episode, rather than making "the abortion episode". Viewers who have spent years with the character take it in as life, not a lecture.
- **When to use:** Social issues, health, politics, anything a buyer fears.
- **How to score (guidance):** 5 if the issue lands through a loved character's choice in a normal episode; 3 if flagged as a special; 1 if a lecture.
- **Quote:** "I wanted people that somebody knew and loved to be going through this and for it not to be the abortion episode." (S015) "I think you can entertain people and at the same time make some points." (S032)
- **Limit:** Early on Grey's she lost her nerve and turned Cristina's abortion into an ectopic pregnancy: "And I got scared." (S015)
- **Sources:** 4 (S015, S032, S038, S042)

## 17. The workplace drama lens *(her term)*
- **What it is:** Every Shondaland show is a workplace drama, even a romance. In Bridgerton the marriage mart is the women's only arena of power, so suitors are colleagues and the ball is a job.
- **When to use:** Finding stakes in a romance, period piece or family show; giving women leads agency.
- **How to score (guidance):** 5 if the characters compete for something with real stakes in a defined arena; 3 if the arena is decorative; 1 if nothing is at stake for them.
- **Quote:** "I love the fact that for these women, the marriage mart is their workplace." (S076) "To me, Bridgerton is a workplace drama. The women have no power in any other areas of their lives." (S050)
- **Sources:** 3 (S050, S068, S076)

## 18. Spinoffs must stand on their own *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Audiences want more of the same, so a spinoff of a character tends to be a weaker copy. She refused Chicago or Boston Anatomy and a Grey's "in a cornfield"; she prefers prequels or a supporting character's private side.
- **Parts:** (1) Is there a new world, not a rebuild? (2) Is there a private side of a public character we have not seen? (3) Would it hold up for someone who never saw the original? (4) Only make it if it can be really good.
- **When to use:** Franchise extensions, sequels, platform requests for "the next Grey's".
- **How to score (guidance):** 5 if the spinoff has its own world, tone and question; 3 if it leans on the original's stars; 1 if it is the original moved to a new location.
- **Quote:** "If you spin off a character, what people want is more of the same." (S080) "But we really tried to look for shows that could stand on their own two feet, that were their own thing." (S080) "We only see her public side, and I really wanted to delve into that." (S095) "I just want it to be really good if we're going to tell it." (S094) To Netflix: "You're not going to get another Grey's Anatomy ... not Grey's Anatomy in a cornfield, Grey's Anatomy on a baseball field or Grey's Anatomy at an airport, that's just not happening," (S078)
- **Sources:** 6 (S032, S078, S079, S080, S094, S095)

## 19. Hire, trust, let go *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Hire people who can run it, then get out of the way. She does not half-supervise: if she watches cuts, she has notes, and then it is no longer the showrunner's show. She hands a show to one successor who shares her sensibility. Leaders let the people they put in charge lead; they do not need to be liked.
- **Parts:** (1) Pick a successor with her sensibility. (2) Stop signing off on scripts. (3) Do not watch early cuts. (4) Be available as the person who talks to the network. (5) When visiting set, praise loudly and leave.
- **When to use:** Running several shows, handing off a long-running series, producing other creators' work.
- **How to score (guidance):** 5 if each show has one clear owner and the founder's role is defined; 3 if the founder still hovers; 1 if nobody knows who decides.
- **Quote:** "If I allow myself to have input, then I'm going to have notes, and if I have notes, then everybody has to take my notes." (S092) "If I watch, I am going to have notes, and then I'm going to have to give you those notes, and then you are going to have to take those notes, and then it's not yours anymore." (S079) "Also, a good leader lets the people they've put in charge lead. They don't need to be liked." (S079) "if you hired people to do a job, you should let them do that job. Like it's not my job to micromanage you." (S101)
- **Sources:** 7 (S013, S019, S079, S080, S092, S101, S011)

## 20. The room you can be locked in with *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** A writers room is intimate; hire writers you can spend hours with who have their own voice and will challenge you. No single voice speaks for a whole group ("My black is not your black").
- **When to use:** Staffing a room, choosing co-writers, building a development team.
- **How to score (guidance):** 5 if the room has distinct voices, people who argue with the showrunner, and range of life experience; 3 if competent but like-minded; 1 if it echoes the showrunner.
- **Quote:** "Writers that you want to be locked in a room with for hours on end are a commodity." (S081) "I'm not looking for writers who sound like me, I'm looking for writers who challenge me." (S081) "And I say it in the writers' room all the time: My black is not your black." (S084) "If you can look around the table and only see yourself, something is wrong with your table." (S063)
- **Sources:** 5 (S027, S063, S067, S081, S084)

## 21. Don't copy, don't bury the lede, paint a picture *(her storytelling lessons)*
- **What it is:** Her three named principles of storytelling. Never redo what you have seen. Show who the lead is in the first seconds. Make images the audience can hold.
- **When to use:** Pilots, cold opens, pitches, loglines.
- **How to score (guidance):** 5 if the pilot's first minutes establish the lead's nature in a fresh, vivid way; 3 if clear but familiar; 1 if derivative or the lead is hidden until late.
- **Quote:** "The one that I adhere to the most is, is if you've seen it before, don't do it again." (S101) "So one, I always say don't copy. Two, I always say people love to have stories where like there's a surprise at the end and that's great, but don't bury the lede ever." (S101) "You know, you watch Scandal, two seconds in, you know exactly who Olivia Pope is and what kind of person she's gonna be, right?" (S101) "And also paint a picture." (S101)
- **Sources:** 2 (S077, S101)

## 22. Overwrite, then rewrite in the edit *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Scripts run long (the Grey's pilot was about 65 pages) so the edit has scenes to choose from. A show is made three times: in writing, in production, and finally in the edit.
- **When to use:** Script length debates, shooting ratios, post schedules.
- **How to score (guidance):** 5 if the plan protects edit time and some extra coverage of key scenes; 3 if tight; 1 if there is nothing to cut to.
- **Quote:** "The real rewrite is in the editing process, so it's always good to have scenes." (S080) "and then you're taking the thing you created and really sort of finally creating it in the editing process" (S074)
- **Sources:** 3 (S073, S074, S080)

## 23. Pitch with the buyer's answer *(unnamed; "What does Bob Iger want?")*
- **What it is:** Find out what the buyer needs, then fit your own vision into it. She asked what the head of the network wanted (a medical show) and put her competitive, sexual, work-obsessed women into surgery. She gives executives the marketing handle up front ("Sex in the Surgery").
- **When to use:** Pitching to a platform, a fund or a co-producer; choosing which of several ideas to bring.
- **How to score (guidance):** 5 if the pitch answers the buyer's stated need without losing the creator's core; 3 if it serves one at the cost of the other; 1 if it ignores the buyer or abandons the vision.
- **Quote:** "I very astutely said, 'What does Bob Iger want, what is he looking for?'" (S109) "It's nice to bring them an answer to that question right at the beginning." (S087) "So for me it wasn't a failure as much as it was, I'm testing out my, my theories of how to get this done." (S101)
- **Sources:** 6 (S015, S073, S081, S087, S101, S109)

## 24. Leave when you can solve it in your sleep *(unnamed; "why am I staying?")*
- **What it is:** When problems that took weeks take minutes and nothing is new, comfort is the reason to stay, and that is the wrong reason. Know your why for going; own a stake in what you make; never enter a negotiation you cannot walk away from.
- **When to use:** Changing buyers or platforms, renewing an overall deal, ending a show, studio strategy.
- **How to score (guidance):** 5 if the move has a clear why, a stake and a walk-away line; 3 if the move is right but the terms are weak; 1 if staying or going out of fear or comfort.
- **Quote:** "I reached a point where I could solve problems in my sleep." (S086) "And if the reason is 'cause it's comfortable, that might not be the right reason." (S101) "It's important to have a stake in what you make," (S086) "the best advice I've ever been given is never enter a negotiation you're not willing to walk away from" (S101)
