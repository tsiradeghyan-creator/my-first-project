# Frameworks: Jason Blum

Built from 97 public sources, 2013 to 2026 (interviews, podcasts, festival talks, a commencement address, trade Q&As). 20 frameworks, most-used first. "Sources" counts distinct sources where the idea appears. Names are his where he named them; otherwise a short name marked *(unnamed)*. The "How to score" lines are guidance for the council built from his own numbers, not quotes.

---

## 1. Scale plus back end ("betting on yourself")
- **What it is:** The core of the Blumhouse system. Director, writers and lead actors work for union minimum (scale) and share in the profits through box-office bonuses. The producer takes no fee up front either. Everyone makes money only if the film works.
- **Parts:** Scale up front for all creative principals; bonuses at fixed box-office thresholds; if you had a quote (say $1M), a hit restores it and then exceeds it; first-timers in a Blumhouse film get no back end until they have been in a hit; pay out fast and visibly.
- **When to use:** Any budget or talent deal. Any time someone wants a large fee before the work exists.
- **How to score:** 5 if every key creative is on scale plus a clear, box-office-linked upside and the producer takes nothing up front; 3 if some fees are up front but most upside is shared; 1 if the budget is mostly fees paid before a frame is shot.
- **Quotes:** "Directors and actors are all paid scale minimum, and then they participate." (S001) "If the movie works, you wind up making a lot more than you would have if you took the money upfront." (S001) "My whole company is built on the notion of betting on yourself." (S058) "If you're in one of our movies for the first time, you don't get any back end." (S004)
- **Sources:** 14 (S001, S004, S008, S013, S014, S016, S018, S058, S072, S076, S088, S097, S108, S111)

## 2. The budget triangle (the three rules of a cheap movie)
- **What it is:** His cost rule for an original film. Three things drive cost: speaking parts, locations, stunts and effects. A director gets one of the three, never more.
- **Parts:** Few speaking parts (a waiter with a line costs hundreds more than a silent extra); few locations (one house is cheapest and where the audience feels most vulnerable); no stunts or effects beyond one or two; then scale plus participation.
- **When to use:** Reading any script or budget. The first test of whether an idea can be made cheaply.
- **How to score:** 5 if the story lives in one or two locations with a small speaking cast and no set pieces; 3 if it needs one category at scale; 1 if it needs two or three (many locations, a big cast and action).
- **Quotes:** "there's kind of a triangle, three things that affect budget - the number of characters, the number of locations and the number of stunts and special effects" (S010) "you can either have a lot of locations, a lot of speaking parts or a couple of special effects, but you can't have you can't have you can't have more than one of those categories" (S014) "Not too many locations. The most economical way to shoot a movie location-wise it to have it take place all inside a house" (S016)
- **Sources:** 7 (S002, S010, S014, S016, S072, S079, S110)

## 3. Recoup if everything goes wrong *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Set the budget at the level that pays back even if the film never gets a wide release, through international sales, VOD and streaming. The flop then costs little and a hit pays for everything.
- **Parts:** A floor of about $4M in 2015 ("two for international and two for domestic"); an original cap that rose over time ($5M in 2013 to 2018, closer to $10M by 2022, $10M to $15M by 2024); sequels and IP allowed more; no guarantee of wide release.
- **When to use:** Setting a budget ceiling; judging whether a project can survive the downside.
- **How to score:** 5 if a realistic worst case (no theatrical, one sale) still covers cost; 3 if the downside loses a little; 1 if the film only works as a hit.
- **Quotes:** "The model is, really, if everything goes wrong, we will recoup." (S072) "Our idea model at Blumhouse is a movie that costs $4 million and change. If everything goes wrong at that price, you're still okay financially" (S019) "now we're closer to ten million dollars than five. So low budget is more expensive." (S057)
- **Sources:** 10 (S008, S019, S024, S025, S027, S050, S057, S062, S072, S088)

## 4. Independent production, studio distribution
- **What it is:** His founding idea. Make films the way independents do (cheap, director-led) and release them the way studios do (wide, with big marketing). Learned from making The Tooth Fairy (miserable production, amazing distribution) at the same time as Paranormal Activity.
- **Parts:** Independent cost structure; a studio partner with a long first-look deal; studio marketing for the films that earn it.
- **When to use:** Deciding whether to self-distribute, sell to a platform, or partner with a big distributor.
- **How to score:** 5 if a cheap, director-led film has a real path to a big distributor; 3 if only a platform sale is possible; 1 if the film must be both produced and distributed small.
- **Quotes:** "I thought the ideal thing would be to make movies independently and have them released by studios." (S005) "It's straddling two worlds; it's straddling production in the independent world and distribution in the studio world." (S073) "I was frustrated with the production process of the studio movie and very impressed with the distribution process of the studio movie." (S004)
- **Sources:** 7 (S004, S005, S013, S072, S073, S089, S019)

## 5. The Sundance test (a drama draped in genre)
- **What it is:** Take out every scare; what is left must be a drama good enough for Sundance. Genre is the skin that gets a personal story into 3,000 theaters.
- **Parts:** A real human story (a father choosing career over family in Sinister, grief in Insidious, a doubted girlfriend in Paranormal Activity); great acting; scares laid on top and organic to the characters.
- **When to use:** Script reads, development notes, judging any genre pitch.
- **How to score:** 5 if the story works with no scares at all; 3 if the drama is thin but the concept is strong; 1 if it is a string of set pieces.
- **Quotes:** "If you take out the genre moments, do you have a story that would play and win the Sundance Film Festival?" (S050) "a good horror movie should survive if you have take out the scares. And just watch it with no scares. It should be a great drama" (S014) "The genre layer of the movies is a way to turn the system on its head and smuggle these dramas into 3,000 theaters." (S072)
- **Sources:** 10 (S004, S013, S014, S020, S050, S072, S075, S078, S113, S001)

## 6. Low budget buys creative risk *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** The reason for the model, in his telling, is freedom, not profit. At $100M every choice must resemble past hits; at $3M you can kill the lead, hire unknowns, and make a film about gun control.
- **Parts:** Small budget, so less pressure; so a concept no one would fund at $20M; so the director can try what studios forbid.
- **When to use:** When someone proposes adding money to "make it better"; when a concept is strange.
- **How to score:** 5 if the budget is low enough that the strangest choice in the script survives; 1 if the budget forces the film to look like three recent hits.
- **Quotes:** "when you make a low-budget movies it's the opposite you could totally take risks you can hire directors who aren't hot you can hire actors who aren't famous" (S013) "That's why I do low budget, because it's low risk. I don't do low budgets because of big profits." (S050) "When you do a movie for a low budget, the pressure to be a financial success goes down exponentially" (S023)
- **Sources:** 11 (S004, S013, S018, S023, S024, S050, S058, S061, S062, S094, S048)

## 7. Final cut for scale talent, notes as a menu
- **What it is:** If you ask someone to work for scale, you must let them make their film. The director gets final cut (outside big-budget work); Blumhouse gives many notes and lets the director choose.
- **Parts:** Final cut to the director on low-budget films; a large house document and actor list offered as options; raise what is not working but never force it; about 20 percent of notes ignored and he lives with it.
- **When to use:** Setting up a director deal; any fight over creative control.
- **How to score:** 5 if the person taking the risk holds the final decision; 1 if a low-paid director is told what to do.
- **Quotes:** "it's immoral to ask someone to work for a reduced rate, but then tell them but I'm going to tell you what to do" (S014) "I guess we offer it as a menu and we let the director choose rather than fighting and arguing about it endlessly." (S073) "The battle of wills, the director always wins, right?" (S060) "when you give them power, they want your notes; it's not a fight" (S050)
- **Sources:** 10 (S014, S019, S025, S050, S052, S060, S066, S072, S073, S075)

## 8. The greenlight questions *(the Monday meeting)*
- **What it is:** His tests for saying yes. Someone at the company must love it; he must love it; it must be scary or unnerving; it must feel new and different. Later he cut it to three micro questions and then to two boxes.
- **Parts:** Love, scary, new. Then can it be made cheaply, and who directs. The opposite of a studio's "it must feel like three recent hits."
- **When to use:** First read of any project.
- **How to score:** Each "yes" counts. Three or four yeses plus a cheap shape is a go; "it feels like something we've seen" is a no regardless of the rest.
- **Quotes:** "someone has to love it I have to love it" (S013) "it has to feel new and different" (S013) "And those are our three questions. And if the answer to those three questions is 'yes,' more likely than not, we're gonna make it." (S026) "So I liked it and it felt different. And so if those two boxes are checked, more often than not, we make it." (S110)
- **Sources:** 9 (S013, S024, S026, S066, S073, S094, S095, S110, S030)

## 9. Moneyball for movies (undervalued, experienced talent)
- **What it is:** Hire on the body of work, not the last film. Prefer directors with several features, some hits, maybe recent flops, who are hungry for freedom. Rarely first-timers on the theatrical slate.
- **Parts:** At least a couple of films he likes; experienced directors waste less (shorter scripts, fewer takes); written-off talent (Wan after two flops, Shyamalan) over the hot new name.
- **When to use:** Choosing a director or key creative.
- **How to score:** 5 for an experienced, undervalued director willing to work for scale; 3 for a first-timer with heavy set experience and a second producer; 1 for a hot name who wants a big fee and no notes.
- **Quotes:** "hollywood looks at your last movie. They don't look at your body of work, which is insane" (S014) "We work very rarely with first-time directors. We're more Moneyball -ish. We use undervalued talent." (S072) "I think when you have a very, very experienced filmmaker, they know how to spend money and make it much more efficient." (S059)
- **Limit he admits:** Works less now because talented people are fully employed (S014, 2021).
- **Sources:** 9 (S011, S014, S024, S025, S059, S061, S072, S095, S002)

## 10. Test screen, then fix the ending
- **What it is:** Screen rough cuts for real audiences, again and again, and adjust. The ending moves an audience more than anything else.
- **Parts:** Read the scores and every card, watch the room, then use your gut; a note from 60 people matters, from two it does not; test once or twice if the film is great, six to eight times if not; original films often under-test.
- **When to use:** Post-production; deciding reshoots; deciding release.
- **How to score:** 5 if there is a plan and budget to test and adjust; 1 if the film goes out unseen by any real audience.
- **Quotes:** "the biggest thing to make a movie test better is changing the ending that has the biggest impact" (S100) "Not so concerned about the scores, but I'm very concerned about watching a movie play with an audience and making tweaks after you do that." (S087) "You should use it as a tool, but it's not supposed to supersede everything else" (S100)
- **Sources:** 8 (S014, S024, S047, S072, S073, S087, S100, S058)

## 11. Decide the release when the film is finished *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Because the budget is low, there is no need to promise theatrical up front. Finish the film, screen it, and let the hook and the room decide: wide theatrical, streaming, or VOD. No limited releases.
- **Parts:** No guarantee of wide release; a single clear hook leans theatrical; hard-to-market films go to streaming; a promise that the film will come out somewhere.
- **When to use:** Any distribution choice; any pitch that needs a commitment before the film exists.
- **How to score:** 5 if the budget lets you choose the release after the cut; 1 if the film only makes sense in one channel and that channel is not secured.
- **Quotes:** "We don't decide how a movie will be distributed until it's finished" (S096) "If there's a real single hook, then that skewers towards theatrical release. If the movies are trickier to market, then that more often than not will tend towards streaming." (S007) "unlike a pilot, I can promise you it'll definitely come out" (S059)
- **Sources:** 7 (S004, S007, S014, S020, S059, S096, S100)

## 12. Originals are pilots; sequels keep the creators
- **What it is:** Never design an original for a sequel. If it hits, treat it like a pilot that got picked up: spend more on the sequel and give up more of the pie to bring back the original writer and director.
- **Parts:** No sequel talk before a hit; sequel budgets roughly double; the original creator must be involved in some way; each sequel related to the first but not a retread.
- **When to use:** Any sequel, spin-off or franchise decision; any plan to replace a creator to save money.
- **How to score:** 5 if the original team returns and has a new angle; 1 if the plan is cheaper new hires on a proven title.
- **Quotes:** "almost like the original movies are pilots" (S072) "To me, sharing the spoils with the people who created the first movie is the secret." (S001) "It's totally black and white. On originals, I never think about what a sequel could be." (S108) "I think the most important factor to creating a successful sequel is to have the people responsible for the original movie back" (S036)
- **Sources:** 10 (S001, S005, S036, S050, S064, S066, S072, S086, S087, S108)

## 13. No fee up front gives you a voice *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** A producer who takes nothing up front can push actors, executives and marketing, because he only wins if the film works. Fees split the team; parity aligns it.
- **Parts:** Producer fee zero; scale across the board; interests of financier and talent aligned.
- **When to use:** Negotiating your own fee; judging whether a partner's incentives match yours.
- **How to score:** 5 if the decision-makers are paid only from success; 1 if they are paid regardless.
- **Quotes:** "I fundamentally believe that your words have so much credibility if you're not taking money up front" (S004) "What I love about low-budget movies is my interests and the director's interests and the actors' interests are aligned." (S051) "Our fee on 'M3GAN' was zero. Our fee on 'Black Phone' was zero." (S097)
- **Sources:** 7 (S004, S051, S072, S076, S097, S111, S062)

## 14. Horror as a Trojan horse
- **What it is:** Horror is the one genre that lets a subversive or personal story into the mainstream system. Gun control (The Purge), race (Get Out), abuse (The Invisible Man).
- **Parts:** A strong genre hook for marketing; the real subject underneath; entertainment first.
- **When to use:** When a filmmaker has a theme that would otherwise get a tiny release.
- **How to score:** 5 if the theme rides inside a hook the audience wants; 1 if the theme is the pitch.
- **Quotes:** "use the mainstream system to do subversive stories. There's no other genre you can really do that with" (S018) "We use the skin of a horror movie to smuggle in movies about gun control, like The Purge , or movies about race, like Get Out" (S020) "it provides a theatrical delivery system for your story" (S029)
- **Sources:** 6 (S018, S020, S029, S031, S062, S113)

## 15. Prove it small, then spend to show it *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Test a concept at the smallest budget; spend more only after the audience says yes. The first Purge stayed inside one house; the sequels showed the streets.
- **Parts:** Original at minimum cost; sequel track with more money because marketing is built in; budget follows proof.
- **When to use:** Scoping a first season, first film, or pilot.
- **How to score:** 5 if version one is the cheapest test of the core idea; 1 if version one already needs the full-scale world.
- **Quotes:** "It was all inside a house. It was strictly because of the budget." (S060) "When you have a sequel or an existing I.P., your marketing is built in. We spend much more money on our sequels." (S027)
- **Sources:** 5 (S025, S027, S060, S078, S108)

## 16. A minute a page; cut before you shoot
- **What it is:** Script length is budget. A 90-minute film needs a 90-page script; every page over 100 is shot and thrown away.
- **Parts:** Cap scripts under 100 pages; do the cutting at script stage; experienced directors shoot what ends up in the film.
- **When to use:** Script lock; schedule and budget review.
- **How to score:** 5 if page count matches target running time; 1 if the script is 110 pages for a 90-minute film.
- **Quotes:** "The truth is, if you want a 90-minute movie, your script should be 90 pages." (S059) "Every time there's a script that's 105 pages, there are 10 pages that we shoot that we don't use." (S059) "I try and never shoot a script that's over a hundred pages for that reason." (S087)
- **Sources:** 2 (S059, S087)

## 17. Old scripts, new alchemy *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Most of his hits were scripts that had kicked around for years. Change one ingredient (a new filmmaker, or a quarter of the budget) and a dead project lives.
- **Parts:** Look for rejected material; re-cost it (The Boy Next Door from $25M to $4M; Ouija from $105M to $5M); attach a filmmaker with a fresh angle.
- **When to use:** Development; reviving a stalled project.
- **How to score:** 5 if a new budget or director plausibly fixes why it stalled; 1 if it stalled because the idea is weak.
- **Quotes:** "Most of the biggest successes at the company are not the new hot shiny object. But they're scripts that had been kicking around town" (S007) "It's not that we're great risk takers, we're doing it for much less money than anyone would do, so we can try weird stuff." (S096)
- **Sources:** 4 (S007, S058, S072, S096)

## 18. Grow by adding a creator, not executives
- **What it is:** Output is capped by quality, not money. To go from four or five theatrical hits a year to six or seven, he merged with James Wan's Atomic Monster rather than hiring more people.
- **Parts:** Blumhouse recognizes and executes other people's ideas; Wan generates ideas; one machine, two labels.
- **When to use:** Any plan to scale volume.
- **How to score:** 5 if growth adds creative judgment; 1 if it adds staff and money only.
- **Quotes:** "It's easy to make more movies, but it's very hard to make more good movies." (S083) "By just throwing around more money and hiring more people, we could make more movies" (S097) "I built a business by recognizing great ideas from other people." (S033)
- **Sources:** 5 (S027, S033, S083, S097, S053)

## 19. Pay out in public; repeat collaborators are the proof *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** Trust is the asset that makes scale deals possible. Pay bonuses immediately, film the checks, and keep working with the same people.
- **Parts:** Transparent box-office triggers; in-house casting head who fronts the deals; directors and actors who come back.
- **When to use:** Building a talent pool on a low budget.
- **How to score:** 5 if there is a record of paying out and repeat partners; 1 if the offer is "trust me" with no history.
- **Quotes:** "it's very easy to get famous people to work for nothing upfront because we've paid we have a great track record of paying" (S013) "The single most important signal to the creative community that you know what you're doing is to have people come back and work with you repeatedly." (S062)
- **Sources:** 5 (S013, S061, S062, S108, S018)

## 20. The event test (2020s theatrical) *(unnamed)*
- **What it is:** His newer filter. Small family-in-a-house scares now play at home; theatrical needs IP, a sequel, or an original that feels like an event.
- **Parts:** Ask why anyone leaves the house for it; IP and sequels carry most of the theatrical market; originals must be perfect.
- **When to use:** Deciding theatrical versus streaming after 2020.
- **How to score:** 5 if the audience would say "I have to see that in a theater"; 1 if "it'll be at home in a few weeks."
- **Quotes:** "I really try to push our filmmakers to make our scary movies feel like events." (S112) "Now the market has to be perfect, and the movies have to be perfect." (S005) "People are going to the things that they know." (S005)
- **Sources:** 6 (S005, S029, S040, S093, S100, S112)
